FILE A42-SE-11 // SPACE & EARTH RECORD

A 2012 planetary alignment was supposed to destroy Earth

The 2012 planetary-alignment doomsday claim was false. Calendar rollover and apparent sky geometry posed no physical threat.

Specific allegation

A doomsday story predicted that planets would align with the Galactic Center or reverse Earth’s poles in 2012, causing earthquakes or solar disruption. The configuration was calculable and no catastrophe followed. Maya calendar scholarship describes a cycle rollover, not a physical prediction of planetary destruction.

Chronology and context

The date corresponded to a completion in the Maya Long Count calendar. Planet positions around it were ordinary apparent alignments, common perspective effects. NASA and astronomers published ephemerides beforehand. Claims shifted among alignments, pole flips, and Nibiru as the deadline approached.

Observations and physical record

Planetary positions are measured precisely; there was no exceptional gravitational arrangement or incoming body. The sky and Earth records show no predicted catastrophe. The Maya calendar’s inscriptions provide historical context, while modern ephemerides test the physical configuration directly.

Established scientific account

Planets can appear close in the sky while being vastly separated in space. Their gravitational effects on Earth are small compared with the Moon and Sun. The Galactic Center is distant and exerts no sudden special influence. Earth’s magnetic field is generated in its core, not switched by a visual line-up.

Limits and contrary evidence

Exact alignments are approximate and depend on reference frame, but that does not create a dangerous force. Earth’s magnetic field changes over time, yet planetary positions do not flip it. The forecast failed its direct test: the date passed without the specified physical effects.

What could change the assessment

A future claim should preregister date, force, and measurable threshold. JPL ephemerides can calculate positions and tides; seismic and geomagnetic data can check effects. Cultural calendar interpretation deserves accuracy, while physical catastrophe requires independent physical evidence.

Ephemerides describe positions in three dimensions, whereas “alignment” usually means objects appear near one another from Earth. The distinction matters: projected geometry does not concentrate planetary gravity. A date-specific disaster forecast should therefore publish force calculations and measurable thresholds, not just a sky chart or an evocative calendar date.

Calibrated conclusion

The 2012 planetary-alignment doomsday claim was false. Calendar rollover and apparent sky geometry posed no physical threat.
