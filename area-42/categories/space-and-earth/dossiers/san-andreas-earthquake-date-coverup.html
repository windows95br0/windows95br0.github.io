FILE A42-SE-064 // SPACE & EARTH RECORD

Scientists know the San Andreas earthquake date but conceal it

California faces significant earthquake risk, but scientists cannot reliably name the next San Andreas earthquake’s date. Published probability estimates are not a secret schedule.

Specific allegation

The specific allegation is that scientists can predict the date of a major San Andreas Fault earthquake but suppress the warning to protect property markets or avoid panic. The falsifiable question is whether a forecast specified a time, place, and magnitude with reliable skill beyond chance. Earthquake hazard probabilities and long-term fault histories are not deterministic predictions.

Scientific and historical chronology

A sequence of moderate Parkfield earthquakes motivated an anticipated repeat near 1988. The USGS and collaborators issued a formal forecast, but the next magnitude 6 event occurred in 2004, outside the predicted window. Modern California assessments estimate probabilities over decades using fault slip, paleoseismology, and earthquake catalogs; they do not provide an exact date for the next “Big One.”

Observations and records

The San Andreas system is measured with seismometers, GPS, satellite radar, and geologic trench records. Fault segments accumulate strain and occasionally release it in earthquakes. Historical recurrence is irregular, and rupture can propagate across segments in ways not predictable from a simple clock. Monitoring data do not show a precursor pattern that identifies a specific future date.

Mainstream explanation and counterevidence

Probabilistic forecasts such as UCERF estimate the chance of earthquakes of specified sizes over a period and region. These estimates support building codes and emergency planning. The Parkfield forecast’s miss is an important cautionary result: even a well-studied fault does not behave like a scheduled event.

Uncertainties and limits

Fault geometry, stress transfer, friction, and sparse prehistoric records limit probability estimates. Risk is real and can change as new earthquakes occur, but no validated method predicts an exact date. A missed forecast or revised hazard model is not proof that a correct secret date exists.

Evidence that could change the assessment

A prediction method would need to make prospective, time-stamped forecasts with defined magnitude and location, demonstrate statistically significant skill over many events, and outperform baseline probabilities. Evidence of a hidden precise forecast would require verifiable internal records plus independently confirmed data supporting it.

Calibrated verdict

California faces significant earthquake risk, but scientists cannot reliably name the next San Andreas earthquake’s date. Published probability estimates are not a secret schedule.
