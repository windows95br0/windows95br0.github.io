FILE A42-SE-068 // SPACE & EARTH RECORD

A Bermuda Triangle gravity or compass anomaly causes disappearances

No credible measurements establish a Bermuda Triangle gravity hole or unique compass defect. The region has ordinary environmental and navigational hazards, while some historical cases remain unresolved.

Specific allegation

The specific claim is that an anomalous gravity field or compass behavior in the Bermuda Triangle pulls ships and aircraft off course, with authorities hiding the physical cause. A test requires measured gravitational or magnetic anomalies that are unusually large, geographically bounded, and correlated with incidents. The popular triangle’s boundaries are not standardized.

Scientific and historical chronology

The “Bermuda Triangle” label became popular in the mid-twentieth century through magazine articles and books aggregating losses from different eras. NOAA and the U.S. Coast Guard have not recognized it as a special hazard region. Modern navigation uses satellite positioning, inertial systems, charts, and updated magnetic models; no unique gravity or compass field has emerged.

Observations and records

Earth’s magnetic declination varies across the globe and changes over time; navigation charts and models account for it. Gravity varies slightly with latitude, elevation, and subsurface density, but there is no documented local anomaly capable of pulling craft or disrupting all compasses. Incident lists often omit weather, vessel condition, route, and cases where craft arrived safely.

Mainstream explanation and counterevidence

The region sees hurricanes, thunderstorms, the Gulf Stream, heavy traffic, and historically uneven reporting. Accidents occur in busy waters worldwide. Ordinary mechanical failures, navigation errors, severe weather, and incomplete records explain incidents without a hidden field. Compass variation is a known navigational quantity, not a Bermuda-specific malfunction.

Uncertainties and limits

Some historic losses remain unexplained because wreckage and reliable records are absent. The triangle’s exact boundaries and incident denominator are often chosen after the fact. A missing vessel is not evidence for a novel force, and lack of a complete accident record limits exact comparisons rather than supporting a cover-up.

Evidence that could change the assessment

A calibrated, independently replicated gravity or magnetic anomaly with a measured magnitude, mapped boundary, and causal link to instrument failure would change the assessment. Incident studies should use predefined boundaries and compare rates per voyage or flight with similar regions and weather conditions.

Calibrated verdict

No credible measurements establish a Bermuda Triangle gravity hole or unique compass defect. The region has ordinary environmental and navigational hazards, while some historical cases remain unresolved.
