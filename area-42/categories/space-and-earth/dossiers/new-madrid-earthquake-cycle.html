FILE A42-SE-066 // SPACE & EARTH RECORD

The New Madrid fault is due for a repeat of the 1811–12 earthquakes

New Madrid is a genuine seismic hazard, but the evidence does not support a fixed 200-year cycle or an imminent scheduled repeat.

Specific allegation

The specific claim is that the New Madrid Seismic Zone follows a fixed cycle and is now due for a catastrophic repeat of the 1811–1812 earthquakes, with authorities minimizing an imminent event. The central test is whether instrumental and paleoseismic data show a dependable recurrence clock. Historical magnitude estimates and prehistoric events carry substantial uncertainty.

Scientific and historical chronology

A sequence of large earthquakes struck the central Mississippi Valley in December 1811 through February 1812. Accounts describe widespread shaking, liquefaction, and changes along the Mississippi. Instrumental seismic networks later documented frequent small earthquakes in the New Madrid region. Modern studies disagree over how persistent the long-term rate is, but no periodic schedule has been established.

Observations and records

The seismic zone has shallow earthquakes and paleoliquefaction features in river sediments. Estimates of the 1811–12 magnitudes and rupture locations are inferred from sparse written accounts and geological effects, not modern instruments. Geodetic measurements show little surface deformation compared with some western faults, although that does not eliminate hazard.

Mainstream explanation and counterevidence

USGS hazard models recognize the potential for damaging earthquakes in the central United States, while the annual probability of a great event is low and uncertain. Earthquake occurrence is not periodic simply because a past event happened roughly two centuries ago. The 1811–12 sequence is a record of hazard, not a calendar.

Uncertainties and limits

Paleoliquefaction interpretation, recurrence intervals, fault segmentation, and the extent of 1811–12 ruptures remain debated. Some studies suggest the present seismicity may reflect aftershock decay; others infer persistent activity. Those competing interpretations affect long-term probabilities, not a known imminent date.

Evidence that could change the assessment

Longer seismic catalogs, improved dating of liquefaction deposits, denser geodetic observations, and a reproducible stress/rupture model could refine regional probabilities. A cycle claim would need multiple well-dated, statistically regular events and out-of-sample predictive success—not extrapolation from one historical sequence.

Calibrated verdict

New Madrid is a genuine seismic hazard, but the evidence does not support a fixed 200-year cycle or an imminent scheduled repeat.
