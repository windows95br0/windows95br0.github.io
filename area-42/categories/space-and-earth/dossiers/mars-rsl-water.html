Recurring slope lineae as hidden flowing water

Recurring slope lineae are observed, but current evidence does not establish flowing water or a cover-up. Dry granular activity explains many cases.

Specific allegation

Dark seasonal streaks called recurring slope lineae were once discussed as possible briny flows. A stronger allegation says agencies found Martian rivers and concealed them. The features are real, but their interpretation changed as images and models improved; many are now explained as dry granular flows.

Chronology and context

Orbital images showed streaks lengthening in warm seasons and fading later. Early studies proposed brines; follow-up observations compared slopes, flow paths, and thermal conditions. NASA publicly revised descriptions as evidence accumulated. No rover has sampled these exact features, and no hidden river discovery was announced.

Observations and physical record

Repeated high-resolution imaging records slope changes but has not confirmed liquid water spectroscopically. Many lineae resemble dry avalanches and grain movement. The seasonal appearance is not unique to liquid flow. Mars orbiters provide surface and temperature context, but remote imagery cannot directly assay a tiny transient brine.

Established scientific account

Low pressure and cold temperatures make persistent surface liquid unstable. Salts may lower freezing points, but sand movement and sublimating frost can darken slopes without liquid water. Dry granular flows fit many observed lineae. No biological or chemical evidence from these streaks establishes water or life.

Limits and contrary evidence

Small brines cannot be ruled out everywhere, and some locations are poorly monitored. That is not evidence of substantial streams, lakes, or suppression. Changing interpretations are documented publicly. Unresolved features warrant better measurements, not a leap from seasonal streaks to a concealed discovery.

What could change the assessment

Combine repeat imagery, thermal data, spectroscopy, and landed analysis with contamination controls. A liquid-flow claim must produce a chemical and physical signature that dry-flow models cannot explain. A cover-up requires separate documentary evidence, beyond uncertainty about interpretation.

RSL classification has evolved with evidence, illustrating that scientific conclusions are revisable. The strongest current dry-flow interpretation applies to many observed examples, not necessarily every dark slope mark. A liquid-water claim still needs direct chemical or physical indicators, because seasonal timing alone is shared by non-liquid processes.

Calibrated conclusion

Recurring slope lineae are observed, but current evidence does not establish flowing water or a cover-up. Dry granular activity explains many cases.
