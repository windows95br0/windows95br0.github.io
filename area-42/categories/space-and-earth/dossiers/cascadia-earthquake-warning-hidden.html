FILE A42-SE-065 // SPACE & EARTH RECORD

A Cascadia megathrust warning was hidden after a secret 1980s prediction

Cascadia has a well-supported megathrust and tsunami hazard, publicly recognized since the 1980s. Evidence does not show an exact secret earthquake date or concealed warning.

Specific allegation

The specific claim is that governments secretly know the date of a Cascadia Subduction Zone megathrust earthquake and have withheld a definitive 1980s warning. The region’s risk is real and supported by geological evidence. The alleged secret date is a different, testable claim: it requires a time-specific forecast with validated predictive skill and documentary evidence of concealment.

Scientific and historical chronology

In 1985, geologist Brian Atwater identified evidence of sudden coastal subsidence and tsunami deposition in Washington. Japanese records of an “orphan tsunami” in January 1700 were later matched to a Cascadia earthquake. Offshore turbidites and coastal marsh records reveal repeated prehistoric events. The hazard and emergency-planning discussion is public; recurrence estimates describe probability, not a countdown.

Observations and records

Cascadia’s Juan de Fuca plate subducts beneath North America. Coastal subsidence layers, tsunami sands, offshore sediment flows, and the 1700 Japanese tsunami provide independent evidence of past great earthquakes. These records constrain recurrence over centuries, but intervals are variable and cannot date the next event to a particular year.

Mainstream explanation and counterevidence

A future large earthquake and tsunami are credible hazards, so building codes, warning systems, and evacuation plans are warranted. USGS assessments use paleoseismic records and fault models to estimate probabilities. The publicly documented 1700 event demonstrates risk, not that scientists can foresee the next rupture precisely.

Uncertainties and limits

Paleoseismic records are incomplete, event sizes vary, and rupture may involve different parts of the margin. Probability estimates depend on assumptions about recurrence and segmentation. A high-impact, uncertain hazard is not evidence of a suppressed exact forecast, and preparedness messaging should distinguish possibility from imminence.

Evidence that could change the assessment

A successful claim of precise predictability would require prospective forecasts that specify time, location, and magnitude and repeatedly outperform probabilistic baselines. A cover-up would require authenticated records of a specific forecast and corroborating data withheld from public safety agencies. New geodetic or seismic precursors could refine, but not automatically pinpoint, risk.

Calibrated verdict

Cascadia has a well-supported megathrust and tsunami hazard, publicly recognized since the 1980s. Evidence does not show an exact secret earthquake date or concealed warning.
