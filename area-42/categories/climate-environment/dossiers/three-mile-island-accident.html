DOSSIER A42-CE-80 // CLIMATE & ENVIRONMENT

Three Mile Island: The 1979 Nuclear Accident and Radiation Cover-Up Claim

The allegation

The claim is that the 1979 partial meltdown at the Three Mile Island nuclear plant released far more radiation than officially reported, and that the Kemeny Commission and Nuclear Regulatory Commission concealed the true public-health impact to protect the nuclear industry.

Timeline

A stuck-open relief valve and operator misreadings caused a partial core meltdown at Three Mile Island's Unit 2 reactor near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania on March 28, 1979. President Carter appointed the Kemeny Commission to investigate; it examined technical causes, regulatory failures and public-health impact together with subsequent NRC and independent epidemiological studies over following decades.

What the record establishes

The Kemeny Commission's public reports, the NRC's own radiation monitoring data, and later independent university-led epidemiological studies (including a Columbia University “Hatch-Susser” cohort study) each measured average off-site public radiation doses from the accident at roughly 1 millirem, compared to about 300 millirem from ordinary annual background radiation exposure in the U.S. — a small fraction of typical yearly exposure.

Counterevidence and alternative explanations

Some residents, journalists and activist researchers have since alleged the NRC and industry understated actual radiation releases and downplayed or suppressed evidence of elevated local cancer rates, citing anecdotal cancer clusters and distrust of an industry-adjacent regulator investigating its own oversight failure. Multiple follow-up epidemiological studies by the NRC, Pennsylvania Department of Health, and independent university researchers found no statistically significant increase in cancer incidence or other measurable health effects attributable to the accident's radiation releases among either area residents or plant workers, consistent across several independently conducted studies using different methodologies over multiple decades.

Uncertainty and limits

The accident was a genuine, serious regulatory and engineering failure — the Kemeny Commission's own report was sharply critical of operator training, instrumentation design and NRC oversight, and prompted major nationwide reforms to reactor safety protocols; that criticism of process failures is separate from, and does not itself establish, a claim that radiation exposure or its health effects were concealed or understated in the released data. The consistent multi-decade epidemiological finding of no measurable radiation-linked health effect is not itself proof that zero radiation-related harm occurred to any individual; it does mean no dataset currently supports a large concealed public-health impact.

Calibrated skeptical verdict

Multiple decades of consistent epidemiological findings across several independently conducted studies support a calibrated verdict of “serious accident, negligible measured public radiation dose, no concealment established.” The claim of concealment specifically would require documentary evidence that measured dose or release data were altered or suppressed, which no released Kemeny Commission or NRC record has been shown to contain.

What could change the assessment

A previously unreleased internal document showing regulators altered or suppressed actual measured radiation-release data, or a new independently replicated epidemiological study using a valid comparison population finding a measurable excess cancer rate tied specifically to the 1979 releases, would change this assessment. Anecdotal cancer-cluster reports without a controlled comparison population do not meet that bar.
