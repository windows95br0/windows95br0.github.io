A42-POL-003 // HISTORICAL CLAIM REVIEW

The Algiers Putsch of 1961

Assessment: The coup attempt was authentic, coordinated, and anti-independence.

The precise allegation

French generals and military units in Algeria attempted a coup against Charles de Gaulle's government in April 1961, opposing his move toward Algerian independence. The conspiracy is documented and openly led by retired generals. Claims that de Gaulle secretly allowed the uprising to consolidate personal power are not established, although the failed coup did strengthen his emergency authority.

Chronology and political context

On April 21, four generals seized control in Algiers and broadcast demands. Some paratroopers joined, while many units and civilians did not. De Gaulle invoked emergency powers and made a televised appeal. The putsch collapsed within days. It followed the January 1961 referendum on self-determination and the 1958 crisis that had brought de Gaulle back to power.

Primary historical record

Military communiqués, court-martial records, government speeches, and the referendum record document planning and response. The OAS later pursued violent opposition to independence, a related but distinct organization and campaign. Official accounts establish the coup leaders but may understate support within the military; later memoirs often rationalize participants' choices.

Corroboration and contrary evidence

French and Algerian histories confirm the coup's leadership and the role of paratroop units. The political context of colonial war and the preceding 1958 crisis is well documented. The fact that emergency powers helped de Gaulle politically is not evidence he engineered the uprising; no reliable planning document or independent witness establishes such a scheme.

What remains uncertain

The degree of support in mainland France, the readiness of military commands to join, and the precise relationship between putsch leaders and later OAS networks remain uncertain. The failed effort was shorter than its organizers expected, leaving little evidence of a fully formed post-coup government.

Calibrated verdict

The coup attempt was authentic, coordinated, and anti-independence. De Gaulle's emergency response benefited his government, but claims that he staged the rebellion are unsupported. The case reflects a real fracture between the state and sections of its military over decolonization, not a simple manipulation by one leader.

What evidence could change this assessment

Released operational orders and private communications between de Gaulle's circle and the generals could clarify any alleged provocation. Independent evidence of a deliberate stand-down or staged broadcast would alter the verdict; absent such records, political advantage after the fact is not enough to establish authorship.
