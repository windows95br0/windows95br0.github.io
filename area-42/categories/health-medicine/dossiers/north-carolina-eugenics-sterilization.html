FILE A42-HM-052 // HEALTH & MEDICINE

North Carolina’s Eugenics Sterilization Program

ASSESSMENT: A state-authorized coercive sterilization program is documented; claims about its reach should use the records.

Educational reference only—not personal medical advice. Claims and conclusions are limited to the evidence cited; consult qualified clinicians and current public-health guidance for personal decisions.

The claim and its context

North Carolina’s Eugenics Board authorized thousands of sterilizations under a state program, often targeting people labeled “unfit” amid discriminatory ideas about disability, poverty, race, and social control. This is a documented public-policy abuse, not evidence that all modern reproductive care is part of a hidden eugenics program.

Historical chronology

The legislature created the Eugenics Board in 1933. The board reviewed referrals and approved operations for decades, including after World War II made the violence of state eugenics especially visible. Sterilizations under the program ended in 1974; the successor Eugenics Commission was abolished in 1977. In 2013 the state created a compensation program for eligible living survivors.

What the record establishes

State archival records and the compensation process document more than 7,600 sterilizations authorized through the program. Black people, women, people with disabilities, and people in state institutions were disproportionately affected in particular periods. Individual case files vary; the state total does not mean every person faced the same coercion or circumstances.

Clinical and scientific context

Eugenic claims treated complex social outcomes as inherited defects and misused heredity to justify state control. Sterilization permanently removes reproductive capacity; consent under institutional authority, poverty, or threatened loss of services can be deeply compromised. These historical practices are not representative of contemporary genetic science or voluntary, informed contraception.

Counterevidence, uncertainty, and calibration

Some referrals were formally approved under procedures described at the time as legal, but legal paperwork did not ensure free, informed consent. Archival counts depend on surviving records and eligibility definitions; undercounting is possible. The evidence supports a broad state program and unequal harm, not every anecdote or an unbroken modern continuation.

What could change the assessment

Newly located board files, consent records, institutional correspondence, or survivor documentation could refine counts and clarify individual circumstances. Claims about a particular person should be tied to case-level evidence and not inferred from statewide patterns alone.
