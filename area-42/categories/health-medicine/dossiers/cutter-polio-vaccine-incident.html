FILE A42-HM-009 // HEALTH & MEDICINE

The 1955 Cutter Polio Vaccine Incident

ASSESSMENT: Documented manufacturing failure, followed by reforms.

The claim and its context

Some 1955 polio vaccine lots contained inadequately inactivated poliovirus, causing paralytic disease and deaths. The claim that this proves intentional contamination or that all polio vaccination is unsafe distorts a severe but bounded manufacturing failure and the subsequent response.

After Salk’s inactivated vaccine was licensed, several manufacturers supplied doses. Cutter Laboratories’ production controls failed to fully inactivate virus. Authorities recalled vaccine and revised testing and oversight, making the incident central to vaccine-regulation history.

What is documented

Paralysis among recipients and contacts and deaths are documented. The failure was not a rumor. Investigations identified production and control problems; there is no credible evidence of deliberate release. Manufacturer error and regulatory oversight are separate questions.

Clinical and epidemiological evidence

Outcomes were assessed through case investigation and surveillance rather than randomized comparison. The event demonstrates the importance of lot testing and manufacturing standards. It does not estimate risk for current inactivated polio vaccines made under revised controls.

Contrary evidence and genuine uncertainty

Historical case counts and causal categories vary, especially for secondary transmission and exposure attribution. Those differences do not erase the link to affected lots. Later safety records should be examined independently rather than presumed perfect or identical to 1955.

Calibrated assessment

This was a real manufacturing disaster and reason for rigorous regulation, not evidence that vaccination is a deliberate poisoning scheme. Transparency about the incident strengthens the case for evidence-based oversight.

A responsible evidence test

Compare product-specific manufacturing controls, lot-release standards, monitoring, and current trials. Do not extrapolate a 1955 failure directly to an individual product today. Ask a clinician or public-health agency about personal decisions.

Source notes and limitations
