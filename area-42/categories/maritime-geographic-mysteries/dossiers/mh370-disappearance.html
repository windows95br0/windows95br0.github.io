DOSSIER M60 // SOUTHERN INDIAN OCEAN // 2014

MH370: The Disappearance of Malaysia Airlines Flight 370

[ MARITIME & GEOGRAPHIC MYSTERIES // AIRCRAFT ]

Event and chronology

Malaysia Airlines Flight 370, a Boeing 777 carrying 239 people, disappeared on March 8, 2014 en route from Kuala Lumpur to Beijing. Military radar tracked the aircraft deviating sharply from its filed flight path and turning back across the Malay Peninsula before heading south; satellite communication “handshake” data from Inmarsat, the last data available, indicated it continued flying for approximately seven hours before running out of fuel somewhere in the southern Indian Ocean.

What later accounts allege

The Malaysian-led investigation, involving accredited representatives from Australia, China, France, the UK, the US and other states under ICAO Annex 13 procedures, released its final safety investigation report in July 2018. The report did not identify a definitive cause but concluded the aircraft's flight path changes involved manual control inputs, and criticized air traffic control communication failures; it explicitly did not assign blame to any individual.

Records, searches and conditions

From 2015 onward, more than 20 pieces of debris confirmed or highly likely to be from the aircraft washed ashore across the western Indian Ocean — Réunion Island, Mozambique, Madagascar, Mauritius, South Africa and Tanzania — with a wing flaperon found on Réunion in July 2015 officially confirmed as belonging to MH370. Ocean-drift modeling of these debris locations is broadly consistent with a crash in the southern Indian Ocean, the search area used in subsequent seabed surveys.

Independent and contrary evidence

The most expensive aircraft search in aviation history, using deep-sea sonar across a mapped southern Indian Ocean search zone based on the satellite handshake data, did not locate the main wreckage or flight recorders despite years of searching by Australian, Malaysian and later a private contractor (Ocean Infinity); as of this writing the aircraft's precise resting place remains unconfirmed, a genuine open technical mystery distinct from the broader disappearance's cause.

What remains unknown

Leading non-conspiratorial theories include a deliberate diversion, possibly by the captain (based on a recovered flight-simulator session on his home computer that plotted a broadly similar path into the southern Indian Ocean, though prosecutors found no direct evidence tying it to the actual flight), or a sudden depressurization/mechanical event incapacitating the crew while the aircraft continued on autopilot (a “ghost flight” scenario). Neither has been proven; unsubstantiated claims of a military shoot-down, hijacking-for-cargo, or remote takeover have no supporting evidence in the recovered debris or investigation record.

Assessment

The final path and cause remain formally undetermined by the official investigation; confirmed debris supports a southern Indian Ocean crash, consistent with drift modeling, but the wreckage itself has not been found. Deliberate-diversion and mechanical-incapacitation theories are the leading non-conspiratorial explanations; no evidence supports military shoot-down, hijacking or exotic disappearance claims.
