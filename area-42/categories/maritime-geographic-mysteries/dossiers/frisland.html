DOSSIER M34 // NORTH ATLANTIC // 16th century

Frisland and the Zeno map controversy

[ MARITIME & GEOGRAPHIC MYSTERIES // MAP ]

Event and chronology

A 1558 map published with a narrative attributed to the Venetian Zeno brothers showed Frisland, a large island south of Iceland, among other North Atlantic features. Its geography influenced later maps, but navigators never confirmed a matching island and the map’s source story remains contested.

What later accounts allege

Claims say Frisland was a real land later erased, or that the Zeno narrative proves an ancient transatlantic voyage suppressed by historians. The map’s apparent detail is treated as evidence independent of its disputed publication history.

Records, searches and conditions

The 1558 map and accompanying text are primary evidence for what the publisher claimed. Comparative scholarship traces similarities to earlier maps and analyzes anachronisms, while later hydrographic surveys show no large island at Frisland’s mapped position.

Independent and contrary evidence

The absence of land on modern charts undermines the literal geography. The map’s derivative relationship to existing cartography offers a non-conspiratorial explanation for its detail. No securely authenticated archaeological or documentary evidence supports the alleged Zeno voyages as printed.

What remains unknown

The identity and intentions of the mapmaker and the extent of any genuine family records remain debated. Historians can distinguish known copying from unknown motives; uncertainty about authorship does not establish a lost island.

Assessment

Frisland is best treated as a phantom island and a case in map transmission. A new authenticated contemporary log or physical evidence from a correctly located landfall would change the case; an old copy of the map would not.
