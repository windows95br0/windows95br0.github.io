DOSSIER M57 // SOUTHERN NORTH SEA // C. 8,000–6,000 YEARS AGO

Doggerland: a landscape lost beneath the North Sea

[ MARITIME & GEOGRAPHIC MYSTERIES // SUBMERGED LANDSCAPE / PREHISTORY ]

Event and chronology

After the last Ice Age, rising seas progressively flooded lowlands that connected Britain with continental Europe. Archaeologists and geoscientists use the name Doggerland for this former landscape beneath the North Sea. Around 8,200 years ago, the Storegga submarine landslide generated a tsunami that reached the region. The question is whether that wave abruptly ended Doggerland or affected only parts of a landscape already being inundated by gradual sea-level rise.

The specific claim

A common dramatic claim is that a single Storegga tsunami suddenly drowned the whole land bridge and its inhabitants. Evidence establishes the tsunami and submerged terrain, but the full-collapse version overstates how uniform and instantaneous the change was across different elevations and habitats.

Physical evidence

Seismic mapping, sediment cores, pollen, microfossils, and artifacts recovered by fishermen and survey teams reconstruct former rivers, coastlines, wetlands, and human activity. Researchers have identified tsunami deposits in parts of the North Sea. The dated sediment record shows a changing landscape and provides a way to compare tsunami effects with longer-term sea-level rise.

Competing explanations and counterevidence

The tsunami may have caused severe local flooding, but its impact depended on topography and distance from the coast. Peer-reviewed work finds evidence of deposits without demonstrating that every inhabited area was wiped out at once. Gradual marine transgression, erosion, and later sediment movement also reshaped and submerged the landscape. In addition, dredged artifacts are often separated from their original archaeological context.

What remains uncertain

The distribution and dating of tsunami deposits remain incomplete, and the precise routes by which people adapted or moved are difficult to trace underwater. The lost landscape is reconstructed from partial cores and remote surveys rather than a continuous, fully excavated prehistoric surface.

Assessment

Doggerland was a real, inhabited landscape progressively inundated as sea level rose; a major tsunami also affected parts of it. Present evidence does not show that one wave instantaneously erased the entire region or its population. Better-dated cores, in-context settlements, and finer bathymetric mapping could clarify the scale and timing of local impacts.
