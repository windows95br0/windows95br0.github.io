DOSSIER M33 // ATLANTIC // 15th century

Antillia: the island that shaped Atlantic maps

[ MARITIME & GEOGRAPHIC MYSTERIES // MAP ]

Event and chronology

Antillia appears on fifteenth-century maps as a large island west of Iberia, before European voyages established the geography of the Atlantic. It was often drawn alongside other speculative lands. Later chartmakers altered or omitted it as exploration produced more reliable coastlines.

What later accounts allege

A modern claim sometimes treats Antillia as a real pre-Columbian refuge deliberately removed from history, or as evidence of a lost transatlantic civilization. Its early map presence is cited as proof of a verified landfall.

Records, searches and conditions

Surviving portolan charts and map catalogs document the feature’s changing shape and position. These are primary sources for European cartographic belief, but no contemporaneous ship log, archaeological site or independently verified settlement confirms an island at the drawn coordinates.

Independent and contrary evidence

The Atlantic geography in early maps included conjectural and inherited features. Later chart refinement and modern bathymetry do not reveal a large island at Antillia’s supposed location. A map can record expectation, cosmology or rumor as readily as measurement.

What remains unknown

The precise origin of Antillia’s name and shape remains debated, and the maps cannot tell us whether an earlier report lay behind it. Evidence for a pre-Columbian voyage would need to be separate from the chart symbol itself.

Assessment

Antillia is established as a cartographic feature, not as a confirmed physical island or lost civilization. A securely dated ship log or archaeological evidence with a demonstrable Atlantic provenance could change that conclusion; map appearance alone cannot.
