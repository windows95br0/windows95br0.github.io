DOSSIER A42-TF-061 // SRI LANKA // 2019

Sri Lanka’s 2019 Easter Attacks and Advance Warnings

Suicide bombings at churches and hotels on Easter Sunday 2019 killed 269 people. A domestic extremist network carried them out. Prior intelligence warnings and institutional failures are documented; deliberate government facilitation is not established.

Assessment: The attacks were carried out by a local Islamist network; authorities failed to act on specific warnings, a serious failure not proof of orchestration.

What is alleged

The core foreknowledge allegation is that Sri Lankan intelligence received specific warnings, including information from India, but senior officials failed to share or act on them. Stronger claims say the government or security services deliberately allowed the attacks to occur for political advantage. Evidence supports catastrophic warning failures; proof of an intentional stand-down is a separate and unestablished claim.

Chronology

On 21 April 2019, coordinated suicide bombings struck churches and hotels in and around Colombo and elsewhere, killing 269 people and injuring hundreds. Indian intelligence had passed warnings identifying a local Islamist network. Sri Lankan officials later acknowledged that warnings reached parts of the security apparatus but were not effectively acted upon. A parliamentary select committee, human-rights bodies, and the Supreme Court examined failures. In 2023, Sri Lanka’s Supreme Court found several high officials, including former President Maithripala Sirisena, responsible for failing to prevent the attacks and ordered compensation.

Evidence

The attacks’ coordinated execution, perpetrators’ identities, and links to the local National Thowheeth Jama’ath network were investigated. The existence of prior warning information is documented by inquiry findings and court proceedings. The Supreme Court’s judgment establishes a serious constitutional and duty failure by named officials. This is compelling evidence of preventable failure and forewarning at an institutional level, not proof that officials knew the precise attack plan and intentionally enabled it.

Alternative explanations and counterevidence

The public record remains incomplete about exactly which officials read each warning, when, and what they understood. Security agencies were fragmented, political leaders were in conflict, and intelligence sharing failed. Some warnings contained substantial detail but did not translate into effective arrests or protective measures. A negligence finding does not decide criminal intent, and failure to act—even a grave one—is not equivalent to ordering or staging the bombings.

Calibrated conclusion and what could revise it

A local extremist network carried out the bombings, and Sri Lankan authorities failed to act adequately on specific warnings. The Supreme Court found officials liable for those failures. The public record does not establish a government false flag or intentional permission to kill civilians. Authenticated communications showing officials deliberately withheld a precise warning, or evidence of operational coordination with the bombers, would materially change that conclusion.

Sources and limits

  1. Supreme Court of Sri Lanka, SC FR 163/2019 judgmentPrimary judicial decision finding officials responsible for failures to prevent the attacks; its findings should not be inflated into a finding of intentional orchestration.
  2. Amnesty International, response to the Easter Sunday attacksHuman-rights organization’s contemporaneous call for investigation and protection; later Supreme Court findings provide the stronger record on official failures.
  3. Reuters, report on the 2023 Supreme Court rulingIndependent report summarizes the ruling and intelligence-warning history; it distinguishes failure to prevent from proof of a plot.
