DOSSIER A42-TF-052 // FINLAND / SOVIET UNION // 1939

The Mainila Incident

The November 1939 shelling at Mainila preceded the Soviet invasion of Finland. The Soviet account accused Finland; historians widely regard the incident as a Soviet provocation, though the exact circumstances and surviving records merit care.

Assessment: The Soviet Union blamed Finland for border shelling before invading; later evidence strongly supports a Soviet staged pretext, while details of the firing remain disputed.

What is alleged

The Soviet government alleged that Finnish artillery killed Soviet soldiers at Mainila and used the incident to justify ending its non-aggression pact and beginning the Winter War. The counterclaim is that Soviet forces staged or fabricated the attack to create a casus belli. This is a historical attribution dispute, not evidence about a contemporary terrorist attack.

Chronology

On 26 November 1939, the Soviet government announced that Finnish artillery had fired on Soviet troops near Mainila, causing casualties. Finland denied responsibility and proposed a joint investigation; it also noted that Finnish guns had been moved beyond range of the alleged site. Moscow rejected the proposal, demanded the withdrawal of Finnish forces, and repudiated the 1932 non-aggression pact. Soviet forces invaded Finland on 30 November. After the war, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev wrote that the shelling had been arranged by Soviet authorities, a retrospective account that has to be weighed alongside archival and diplomatic evidence.

Evidence

The close timing between the accusation and invasion, the Soviet refusal of a joint inquiry, the location and range of Finnish batteries, and the absence of independently verified evidence for the Soviet account undermine the original charge. Khrushchev’s memoir supports Soviet responsibility. Finnish and international historians generally treat Mainila as a Soviet provocation, consistent with Moscow’s broader effort to pressure Finland.

Alternative explanations and counterevidence

The public documentary record is incomplete, and the precise firing position, casualties, and chain of command have not been established by a neutral contemporaneous investigation. Khrushchev wrote decades later and was not a witness to every operational detail. Lack of corroboration for the Soviet allegation is not, by itself, a complete reconstruction of who fired each round. These limits affect detail more than the broader conclusion that the Soviet narrative was unreliable and served as a pretext.

Calibrated conclusion and what could revise it

The best-supported conclusion is that the Soviet Union engineered or exploited the Mainila episode to justify its invasion; the claim that Finland launched the attack is not credible on the available record. The precise mechanics remain less certain than the political use. Newly opened Soviet military logs, casualty records, or independently authenticated orders would refine or revise attribution.
