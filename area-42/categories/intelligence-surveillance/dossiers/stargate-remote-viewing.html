FILE STARGATE/REMOTE/VIEWING // 1970s–1995

U.S. Remote-Viewing Program STAR GATE

Agencies funded experiments in purported remote viewing. Some sessions were judged anomalous, but an independent review found them too vague and inconsistent for reliable intelligence use.

ASSESSMENT

Government experiments are documented; operational predictive value was not established.

EVIDENCE FIRST
UNCERTAINTY STATED

CASE ANALYSIS

What the record supports

CLAIM AND BOUNDARY

U.S. agencies funded research into remote viewing, the claimed ability to describe distant targets without ordinary senses. Program files are genuine. Selected sessions include seemingly accurate details, but evidence does not establish a reliable, deployable intelligence method that outperformed ordinary analysis.

CHRONOLOGY AND CONTEXT

Research began at Stanford Research Institute in the early 1970s with Army and intelligence sponsorship under changing names. Agencies later consolidated work under STAR GATE. In 1995, CIA commissioned an American Institutes for Research evaluation, which recommended ending operational use because practical value was not demonstrated.

DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE

Declassified protocols, transcripts, tasking records, and the 1995 AIR report are primary evidence. Reviewers noted some statistically unusual experimental results but found descriptions vague and inconsistent, with little demonstrated usefulness to intelligence decisions. This distinction between laboratory anomaly and operational utility is central.

CONTRARY EVIDENCE AND ALTERNATIVES

Advocates cite striking sessions and argue conventional methods undervalue anomalous perception. Critics point to selective reporting, flexible scoring, cueing, and hindsight. A memorable match cannot establish a repeatable mechanism or prove analysts made better decisions than chance.

UNCERTAINTY

Target records and scoring histories are incomplete, and retrospective evaluation can suffer from hindsight. Statistical anomalies and actionable value are separate questions. The program’s existence establishes official interest and expenditure, not scientific validation or successful intelligence outcomes.

CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT

STAR GATE was a documented research program, but evidence did not justify continuing operational use. The balanced assessment recognizes unresolved experimental anomalies while noting reviewers found no reliable actionable value. Claims of proven psychic spying exceed evidence, as do assertions that every test was fraudulent.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS

Independent, blinded, pre-registered replication with full target records could change the scientific assessment. Predictive accuracy across new targets would matter. Additional anecdotes or selected successes would not; methods must include misses, scoring rules, and practical outcomes.
