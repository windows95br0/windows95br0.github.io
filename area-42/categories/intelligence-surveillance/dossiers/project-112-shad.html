FILE PROJECT/112/SHAD // 1962–1973

Project 112 and Project SHAD

DoD conducted tests at land sites and aboard ships using agents and simulants. Veterans were often not informed; the record does not support claims that every participant was secretly infected with a lethal pathogen.

ASSESSMENT

Classified exposure tests and disclosure failures are documented; outcomes and rosters remain incomplete.

EVIDENCE FIRST
UNCERTAINTY STATED

CASE ANALYSIS

What the record supports

CLAIM AND BOUNDARY

Project 112 and shipboard SHAD tested chemical and biological agents or simulants to assess military vulnerabilities. Some servicemembers were exposed without adequate disclosure. Historical evidence does not show that every participant was secretly infected with a lethal pathogen or that an outbreak has been traced to the tests.

CHRONOLOGY AND CONTEXT

The Department of Defense conducted land and shipboard tests during the Cold War. Tests used agents, simulants, and dissemination methods to study detection and protection. Many participants were not informed then. Veteran inquiries led DoD to release test names, ships, and some rosters in the 1990s and 2000s.

DOCUMENTARY EVIDENCE

DoD fact sheets, GAO investigations, Navy files, and VA reviews document test agents and locations. Released lists have been corrected as additional records surfaced. The sources establish testing and disclosure failures, but not every exposure or health outcome; independent scientific reviews examine the limited medical evidence.

CONTRARY EVIDENCE AND ALTERNATIVES

DoD has said many tests used simulants and that some agents had low persistence under test conditions. This does not establish harmlessness or adequate consent. Veterans’ concerns are real, but temporal association alone cannot tie illness to a specific trial; tests differed in agent, dose, and exposure route.

UNCERTAINTY

Rosters and records remain incomplete, and simulant names are sometimes mistaken for live agents. Some exposures may be undercounted. Health studies have limited statistical power, and long latency complicates causation. New releases can revise detail without validating a broader claim of universal infection.

CALIBRATED ASSESSMENT

Project 112/SHAD involved secret exposure testing and poor disclosure, a serious breach of trust. Conclusions must be test-specific: not every event used a live pathogen or caused illness. Transparency and medical follow-up are warranted because the evidence remains incomplete.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS

Updated rosters, laboratory logs, and manifests could identify exposures. Strong causal claims need dose reconstruction and epidemiology. Verified presence proves opportunity, not disease or cause; a missing record should prompt research, not presumption about what was released.
