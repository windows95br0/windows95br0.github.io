FILE A42-HS-011 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Cato Street Conspiracy

ASSESSMENT: A documented plot to kill the British cabinet; state handling invites scrutiny.

In 1820 Arthur Thistlewood and associates planned to assassinate ministers at a cabinet dinner and trigger revolt. Police informant George Edwards penetrated the group, and authorities arrested conspirators at Cato Street. The plot was real, though infiltration, informant incentives, and the state’s response remain debated.

Claim and historical context

The group met in a London stable loft while Britain faced postwar distress and the Peterloo aftermath. Their plan targeted cabinet ministers gathered for dinner. On 23 February, police raided the premises; Thistlewood escaped briefly, while several conspirators were arrested.

Chronology and primary record

Trial transcripts and Home Office records document the raid and prosecution. Edwards’s information helped police identify the group, but his conduct and compensation have prompted historical questions. The surviving papers show surveillance and a planned attack, not merely a later official claim.

What the record establishes

Five men were executed and one transported. The prosecutions relied on informant testimony and evidence from the raid; historians examine whether informants encouraged violent talk or simply reported it. There is no proof that ministers secretly designed the plot.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

Officials used the conspiracy to justify stronger measures against radicalism, and the political climate constrained dissent. That post hoc utility does not prove the attack was staged. Conversely, a genuine plot does not make every police tactic or sentence proportionate.

Calibrated verdict

The case is best classified as a genuine assassination conspiracy met by infiltration and harsh punishment. Its record is a useful comparison for how governments can exploit real threats without having manufactured them.

Evidence that could change this assessment

An authenticated instruction ordering Edwards to provoke a specific attack would change judgments about state entrapment. It would not erase the conspirators’ independent planning evidence. New Home Office files should be tested against the trial record and informant payment chronology.
