FILE A42-HS-002 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Donation of Constantine

ASSESSMENT: A medieval forgery; no fourth-century imperial grant.

The “Donation” purports to show Emperor Constantine transferring western imperial authority to Pope Sylvester I. The document became an important political prop in later jurisdictional disputes, but textual and historical evidence identifies it as a forgery composed centuries after Constantine’s reign.

Claim and historical context

The surviving text claims Constantine gave the pope primacy, Rome, and extensive western territories after his conversion. It likely emerged in the eighth century, when papal authority and Frankish politics were changing—not in the fourth-century imperial chancery.

Chronology and primary record

Its Latin vocabulary and institutions do not fit Constantine’s era. Lorenzo Valla’s 1440 philological examination exposed anachronisms, including terminology and forms of government that developed later. Valla’s critique survives as a dated scholarly argument rather than a modern rumor.

What the record establishes

Medieval papal use of the document is documented, but the exact path of its composition and transmission remains debated. A text can have real political influence while being false as a contemporary record; later citations do not repair its provenance.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

Some accounts describe a deliberate papal cover-up. Evidence instead shows a forged charter circulating within a broader world of contested legal texts. Historians debate who produced it and for what immediate purpose; they do not treat Constantine’s alleged grant as authentic.

Calibrated verdict

The case is best understood as a successful political forgery, not proof of a continuous secret society. Its later use reveals how documentary authority could shape claims to power. Valla’s method also illustrates how language and institutional history can test a document.

Evidence that could change this assessment

An early, securely dated manuscript with a traceable fourth-century chain of custody would change the authenticity debate. None is known, and the linguistic anachronisms remain decisive. New evidence about the eighth-century author could refine motive without restoring the alleged grant.
