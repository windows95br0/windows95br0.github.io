FILE A42-HS-055 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

Operation Northwoods: A Proposal, Not an Operation

ASSESSMENT: Joint Chiefs staff proposed deceptive pretexts for possible Cuba intervention; the proposals were not approved or executed.

A 1962 U.S. Joint Chiefs memorandum proposed staging or simulating incidents to justify military action against Cuba. The proposal is authentic and disturbing. It was not an executed operation, and available records show civilian leadership did not approve it.

Claim and historical context

The claim is that U.S. military planners considered false-flag or simulated incidents—including attacks on U.S. targets—as potential justifications for intervention. Some retellings wrongly present the memorandum as proof that such attacks were carried out.

Chronology and primary record

The Joint Chiefs sent the memorandum to Defense Secretary Robert McNamara on 13 March 1962 during planning for Cuba. It was not adopted. President Kennedy dismissed Chairman Lyman Lemnitzer later that year. The record became public after declassification in the 1990s.

What the record establishes

The authentic memorandum, titled “Justification for U.S. Military Intervention in Cuba,” survives in declassified records. It lists proposed courses of action and identifies military control and public messaging needs. Its classification history and institutional provenance are documented.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

A planning paper demonstrates that options were considered, not that they were authorized, implemented, or endorsed by the President. The document’s proposals were not policy. Conflating Northwoods with later unrelated incidents erases the distinction between an alarming proposal and an actual event.

Calibrated verdict

High confidence that senior military staff proposed deceptive options; high confidence that Northwoods was not carried out. The case warrants scrutiny of civilian oversight and military planning, but does not substantiate claims that the proposed incidents occurred.

Evidence that could change this assessment

Evidence of an approval order, implementation log, or corroborating operational record would alter the conclusion about execution. The surviving chain instead places the memo among rejected planning options; any new claim must establish an operational link, not merely cite the memo.
