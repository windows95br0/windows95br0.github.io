FILE A42-HS-035 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

“Thuggee” and the Colonial Secret-Society Narrative

ASSESSMENT: Some organized robberies were real; British officials exaggerated a unified hereditary cult.

British colonial authorities described “Thuggee” as a vast hereditary society of ritual stranglers operating across India. Organized robbery and murder existed, but the colonial image of a single ancient cult was shaped by coercive policing and sensational reports. Historians dispute the scale and category imposed by British officials.

Claim and historical context

From the 1830s, William Sleeman led operations that arrested and prosecuted people accused of belonging to Thuggee. Confessions, informer testimony, and state reports produced a large archive. These records document colonial campaigns, but their evidentiary conditions included pressure and incentives.

Chronology and primary record

Officials classified diverse crimes and travelers under a common label and described an inherited religious organization. British courts relied heavily on approvers who testified in exchange for leniency. The archive reflects both criminal activity and the colonial state’s methods of producing knowledge about it.

What the record establishes

Some scholarship accepts the existence of groups that coordinated robbery and murder, while rejecting the claim of one uniform all-India cult. Recent work examines how British texts converted scattered practices into an administratively legible conspiracy and justified expanded policing.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

The opposite claim—that all cases were invented—is also too broad. There were victims and prosecutions, and individual acts can be assessed. But colonial statistics and confession narratives cannot be treated as neutral measures of a timeless, centrally organized sect.

Calibrated verdict

The verdict distinguishes real violence from an overstated conspiracy category. The British state created a durable image of a secret hereditary menace; the record does not establish the vast, uniform organization described in colonial propaganda.

Evidence that could change this assessment

Independent local court records, victim accounts, and material evidence could refine the scale and organization of particular groups. Evidence collected under interrogation should be corroborated rather than counted as independent confirmation of the official category.
