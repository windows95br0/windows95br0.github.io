FILE A42-HS-012 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

Gregory MacGregor’s Poyais Scheme

ASSESSMENT: A documented colonial land fraud, not a functioning hidden state.

Scottish adventurer Gregory MacGregor sold bonds and land claims for Poyais, a fictional Central American state he represented as prosperous. Investors and settlers were deceived in the 1820s. The case is a coordinated promotional fraud involving invented institutions and maps, not evidence that a real nation was erased.

Claim and historical context

MacGregor styled himself “Cazique of Poyais,” issued elaborate descriptions, and persuaded British buyers to invest in bonds and land. Settlers arrived on the Mosquito Coast to find no developed capital or services. Many died from disease and hardship; survivors returned to expose the deception.

Chronology and primary record

Contemporary prospectuses, maps, bond certificates, and press coverage allow scholars to compare promises with the landscape. London’s financial and social networks gave MacGregor credibility. His repeated promotion after the first expedition failed demonstrates persistence, though precise knowledge and complicity varied among intermediaries.

What the record establishes

MacGregor faced legal scrutiny but escaped lasting punishment and later resumed similar schemes elsewhere. Historians debate why elite audiences accepted the fiction and how much responsibility fell on brokers who repeated his claims. No archival map identifies Poyais as a sovereign polity.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

The exotic “lost country” explanation is contradicted by local geography and Indigenous political histories. Poyais was a fabricated investment project. MacGregor’s aristocratic credentials, diplomatic presentation, and printed materials explain the fraud’s reach better than a cover-up by cartographers.

Calibrated verdict

This was a real, consequential deception that exploited colonial assumptions and weak financial verification. It should not be conflated with the Miskito polity or British protectorate history. The case shows that detailed paperwork can manufacture credibility without underlying reality.

Evidence that could change this assessment

A contemporaneous independent census, treaty, or administrative record proving MacGregor’s sovereign government would alter the conclusion. None exists. New evidence about individual promoters could clarify their culpability, but not make the advertised capital or public institutions real.
