FILE A42-HS-039 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Wilmington Coup of 1898

ASSESSMENT: A white-supremacist coup and subsequent cover-up are documented.

In 1898, armed white supremacists overthrew Wilmington, North Carolina’s elected biracial government, destroyed the Black-owned Daily Record, and forced Black residents from public life. Officials long described the event as a race riot. The evidence supports a coordinated coup and decades of historical minimization, not merely spontaneous unrest.

Claim and historical context

The Democratic campaign used racist propaganda and threats before the election. On 10 November, a white mob attacked the Daily Record office, expelled political opponents, and imposed a new government. Armed groups killed and displaced Black residents, though exact death and exile totals remain uncertain.

Chronology and primary record

Contemporary newspapers, correspondence, election data, and the 1898 state investigation document planning and violence. The mob’s white declaration of independence and coordinated political actions contradict later portrayals of an accidental clash between equal groups.

What the record establishes

A 2006 North Carolina commission report synthesized archival and oral evidence, establishing the coup’s political dimensions. Historians debate specific casualty counts and individual responsibility because many victims were not recorded and survivors fled under threat.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

For generations, local and state narratives minimized the overthrow and blamed Black residents. That erasure is documented in textbooks, commemorations, and official histories. The uncertainty in casualty figures should not be used to deny the coup or its racial purpose.

Calibrated verdict

The verdict is a successful coup d’état by white supremacists with a documented historical cover-up. Evidence supports coordinated preparations and state capture. Researchers should state uncertainty in deaths and displacement rather than repeat the earlier “riot” framing.

Evidence that could change this assessment

Newly located private letters, census comparisons, and burial evidence could refine participation and losses. A claim that the transfer was legitimate must account for the armed expulsion, election intimidation, and contemporaneous declarations.
