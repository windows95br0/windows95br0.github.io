FILE A42-HS-005 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Zinoviev Letter

ASSESSMENT: A forged or manipulated political document; no proof of a Comintern directive.

A 1924 letter attributed to Grigory Zinoviev urged British communists to intensify agitation. Its publication days before a UK election helped discredit Labour. The surviving record supports serious questions about authenticity and political handling, but does not establish one proven master plot directing every participant.

Claim and historical context

The letter reached the Foreign Office through intelligence channels and was published by the Daily Mail on 25 October 1924. The timing, four days before the general election, made it politically explosive. Labour lost office, but its defeat had multiple causes.

Chronology and primary record

British cabinet and Foreign Office papers document circulation, debate, and publication. Soviet and Comintern records have not yielded a securely authenticated original. The National Archives’ file trail shows how officials handled the copy; it does not itself prove who composed it.

What the record establishes

Historians have variously attributed the text to anti-Bolshevik émigrés or treated it as a likely forgery. Later official inquiries found no conclusive proof that the Foreign Office knowingly fabricated it. Questions remain about the route through which officials accepted and released it.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

The letter’s language and context cast doubt on genuine Comintern authorship, while absence of an original constrains certainty. No reliable evidence proves the often-repeated claim that intelligence chief Stewart Menzies personally manufactured it. Election influence is plausible; decisive electoral causation is unproven.

Calibrated verdict

The calibrated conclusion is probable forgery or unauthenticated political document, followed by damaging official disclosure. This is a real episode of partisan exploitation and weak verification, not a fully documented conspiracy with a settled author and command chain.

Evidence that could change this assessment

A verifiable original in Comintern records, or contemporaneous correspondence tracing composition and deliberate alteration, could settle the question. Researchers would need custody records and handwriting/material comparison. Additional proof of timing alone would strengthen suspicion, not attribution.
