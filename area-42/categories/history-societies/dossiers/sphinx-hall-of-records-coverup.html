FILE A42-HS-026 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Sphinx “Hall of Records” Cover-Up

ASSESSMENT: No archaeological evidence supports a hidden ancient archive beneath the Sphinx.

A popular claim holds that a library beneath the Sphinx preserves lost technology and is being concealed by Egyptian authorities. Geophysical surveys and excavation records have not identified such a chamber. Subsurface anomalies require interpretation; they are not proof of a sealed archive or its suppression.

Claim and historical context

The “Hall of Records” idea gained modern prominence through psychic Edgar Cayce’s predictions, not an ancient Egyptian text or excavation report. Later retellings placed a library under the Sphinx’s paws, blending esoteric claims with real archaeological interest in the plateau.

Chronology and primary record

Egyptian authorities and research teams have conducted documented surveys and conservation work around the monument. The Sphinx has known cavities and structural fissures, but published archaeological records do not identify a large hidden repository or its contents.

What the record establishes

Geophysical instruments can detect density contrasts, voids, and geological variation; they cannot identify a library without excavation and material evidence. Scholars have used noninvasive methods to protect the monument, which limits what can responsibly be inferred from an anomaly.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

Some advocates cite restricted access as proof of concealment. Conservation controls and national heritage law explain why intrusive digging is regulated. No authenticated inventory, ancient map, or excavation record supports the specific hall described by Cayce followers.

Calibrated verdict

The alleged archive remains unsupported. The real history concerns modern esoteric movements, monument conservation, and responsible limits on excavation. The existence of unresolved subsurface features does not confirm a chamber, much less establish deliberate suppression.

Evidence that could change this assessment

A published excavation with a secure stratigraphic context, independently reviewed architectural evidence, and a verifiable ancient archive would change the assessment. A geophysical signal alone would justify further study, not a conclusion about hidden books or official motive.
