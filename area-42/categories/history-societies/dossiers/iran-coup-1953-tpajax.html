FILE A42-HS-065 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

Iran’s 1953 Coup and Operation TPAJAX

ASSESSMENT: U.S. and British intelligence participated in the coup that removed Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh; operational details continue to emerge.

In August 1953, a coup removed Iran’s elected prime minister, Mohammad Mosaddegh, and strengthened Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. Declassified CIA records confirm U.S. participation in Operation TPAJAX and British involvement. The record clarifies responsibility while leaving local political dynamics central.

Claim and historical context

The core claim is that foreign intelligence agencies organized and supported the coup after Mosaddegh nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. The coup involved Iranian military and political actors too; describing it as solely a foreign operation erases domestic alliances and resistance.

Chronology and primary record

After the 1951 oil nationalization and a 1952 political crisis, U.S. and British officials planned intervention. The first royal decree failed on 15 August 1953. A second effort succeeded on 19 August; Mosaddegh was arrested and the Shah returned from exile.

What the record establishes

CIA internal histories and declassified operational papers acknowledge planning, propaganda, payments, and coordination with Iranian actors. U.S. diplomatic records and Iranian histories establish the sequence. The 2013 release of a CIA history made the agency’s role explicit.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

The exact weight of covert action versus Iranian opposition, military defections, and Shah’s supporters remains debated. Declassified U.S. sources reveal U.S. actions but are not a complete Iranian record. “Coup” is established; claims of total control by foreign agents over every participant are too simple.

Calibrated verdict

Very high confidence that U.S. and British intelligence helped organize and fund the successful coup. High confidence that Iranian actors were essential to its execution. The operation’s precise causal weight and internal Iranian motivations remain matters of historical interpretation.

Evidence that could change this assessment

Further Iranian and British archives, operational accounts, or verified financial records could better establish domestic networks and external direction. Any revision would need to account for the CIA’s own acknowledgment and the documented sequence of events.
