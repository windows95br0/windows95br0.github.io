FILE A42-HS-053 // HISTORY & SOCIETIES

The Gleiwitz Incident: A Staged Border Attack

ASSESSMENT: The SS staged a false-flag incident at Gleiwitz as part of the pretext for Germany’s invasion of Poland.

On 31 August 1939 SS operatives staged attacks along the German–Polish frontier, including a radio-station seizure at Gleiwitz. The operation is supported by postwar testimony and trial records. It was a propaganda pretext, not evidence that Poland initiated the war.

Claim and historical context

The claim is that Nazi Germany fabricated border incidents to portray Poland as the aggressor and justify an invasion already planned. SS officer Alfred Naujocks later described leading the Gleiwitz operation and using a murdered prisoner as a supposed Polish attacker.

Chronology and primary record

Hitler set the invasion date in August 1939. On the night of 31 August SS teams staged several frontier incidents; German forces invaded Poland the next morning. Naujocks recounted the radio-station action in a sworn postwar statement. German occupation and mass violence followed.

What the record establishes

Naujocks’s affidavit entered the Nuremberg record, and the International Military Tribunal examined the staged-incident campaign. The timing, orders to create border pretexts, and German invasion plans corroborate the account. The surviving record is stronger for the operation’s existence than for every operational detail repeated in later retellings.

Competing interpretations and counterevidence

Naujocks was a participant describing his own role after the war and had incentives to minimize or shape responsibility. Some vivid details—especially the identity and exact handling of victims—are less securely established than the broader staging. These qualifications do not restore the Nazi claim of Polish aggression.

Calibrated verdict

High confidence that German security services staged the Gleiwitz incident within a broader false-flag campaign. The precise sequence and individual responsibilities remain subject to source criticism; the incident cannot be used to imply that Germany’s invasion was a defensive response.

Evidence that could change this assessment

A surviving contemporaneous operational log, authenticated orders, or independently corroborated witness testimony could refine the details. Evidence against Naujocks’s account would need to explain the trial record and the established planning of the invasion.
