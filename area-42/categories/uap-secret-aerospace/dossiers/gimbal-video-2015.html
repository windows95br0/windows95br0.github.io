UAP-056 // CASE REVIEW

“Gimbal” Navy infrared video (2015)

Authentic video confirms an observation, not the claimed rotation, performance, or origin.

The precise claim

A 2015 Navy infrared clip appears to show an object rotating and moving unusually; online claims treat the apparent rotation as proof of advanced propulsion.

Chronology and provenance

The video was recorded by a Navy F/A-18 crew off the U.S. East Coast in 2015 and circulated publicly in 2017. The Department of Defense later confirmed the video was authentic Navy footage. Public confirmation did not release all sensor metadata or identify the object.

Public records and observations

DoD authentication establishes that the clip is genuine military footage, while the Navy and ODNI describe UAP observations and data limitations. The short infrared recording documents a sensor image with audio, not a complete measurement of target distance, shape, or motion. The apparent rotation may involve the camera system as well as the target.

Evidence limits

The public clip is cropped and lacks full raw sensor data, calibration, range, aircraft attitude, and all mission context. Apparent size and movement in a rotating infrared camera cannot be interpreted as the object’s physical rotation without instrument geometry and target range. Audio exclamations are observations, not engineering measurements.

Conventional alternatives

A distant aircraft and camera/gimbal rotation are plausible conventional explanations discussed by analysts; the available public clip cannot determine the target conclusively. Optical perspective and sensor motion should be separated before making performance claims.

Calibrated skeptical conclusion

The video is authentic; the extraordinary interpretation is not authenticated. Public evidence does not prove a rotating craft, extreme acceleration, or non-human technology.

What could change this assessment?

Release of original full-resolution sensor files, gimbal and aircraft telemetry, calibrated range, and synchronized radar data would permit an independent motion reconstruction.

Sources and further reading

  1. Department of Defense, Navy-video authentication (2020)Confirms the clips are authentic Navy videos while explicitly leaving the phenomena unidentified.
  2. ODNI Preliminary Assessment: UAP (2021)Summarizes reporting and sensor limits; it does not claim non-human attribution.
  3. NASA UAP Independent StudyExplains requirements for calibrated and reproducible scientific interpretation of sensor imagery.
