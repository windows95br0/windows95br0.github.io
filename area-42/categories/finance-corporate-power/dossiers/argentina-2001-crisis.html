FILE FCP-29 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Argentina’s 2001 default and collapse of the currency board

ASSESSMENT: Policy errors and severe social costs are well documented; a deliberate plan to impoverish Argentina is unsupported.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether IMF policy and the peso-dollar peg prolonged recession and made the eventual default more damaging.

The currency board tied monetary credibility to dollar convertibility, but left fiscal federalism, external borrowing and bank balance sheets exposed to a prolonged recession. Its rigidity made adjustment politically costly; abandoning it also damaged holders of peso and dollar-linked claims.

Context and timeline

Argentina’s convertibility law fixed the peso to the dollar in 1991. Recession, debt and capital flight grew late in the decade; a bank withdrawal freeze began in 2001, sovereign default followed in December, and the peg ended in 2002.

IMF surveillance and lending decisions are assessable against forecasts available at each review, not just the eventual collapse. A program may have delayed recognition of insolvency, but that is analytically different from intending default or planning losses for depositors.

Primary records and formal findings

The IMF Independent Evaluation Office reviewed program decisions and warning signs; the government’s default and devaluation are documented outcomes, but not proof of one actor’s plan.

Default, deposit restrictions and devaluation were distinct policy events with different winners and losers. Any causal account should state which one it explains, and compare contemporaneous debt sustainability projections with later realized growth and exchange-rate outcomes.

Independent economic evidence

Martin Feldstein and Ricardo Hausmann’s research and IMF macroeconomic series analyze peg rigidity, debt sustainability and competitiveness.

A causal account must keep the convertibility regime, sovereign default and deposit restrictions analytically distinct. Each had different triggers and different exposure for workers, savers, banks and foreign creditors.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Austerity and external financing operated alongside domestic fiscal federalism, dollarized liabilities and shocks. No single policy explains all distributional losses or the timing of default.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare debt projections and program reviews with growth, reserves and capital outflows. Test whether alternative adjustment paths were financially feasible and when officials knew the peg was untenable.

Assessment: Policy errors and severe social costs are well documented; a deliberate plan to impoverish Argentina is unsupported.
