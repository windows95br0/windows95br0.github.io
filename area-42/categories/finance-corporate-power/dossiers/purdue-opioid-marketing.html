FILE FCP-50 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Purdue Pharma opioid marketing and OxyContin claims

ASSESSMENT: Misbranding and deceptive practices were admitted in defined cases; attributing the entire opioid epidemic to one company is unsupported.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Purdue Pharma deceptively marketed OxyContin and downplayed addiction risks, contributing to opioid harms.

The 2007 federal plea involved Purdue Frederick and particular misbranding representations; later 2020 corporate resolutions concerned additional offenses and civil claims. Keep each plea’s admitted conduct distinct from allegations that were resolved through negotiated settlements.

Context and timeline

Purdue promoted OxyContin from 1996, with sales tactics targeting prescribers. State and federal investigations examined risk claims, diversion and monitoring; Purdue pleaded guilty to federal offenses in 2007 and again in 2020 as the opioid crisis expanded.

Overdose patterns changed across prescription opioids, heroin and synthetic opioids. CDC surveillance helps date those waves, while epidemiological studies assess prescribing and subsequent harms; neither supports attributing the entire national epidemic to one firm.

Primary records and formal findings

DOJ plea records establish specified misbranding and kickback conduct, and state investigations documented marketing practices. Civil settlements resolve broader claims and should not be represented as adjudications of every allegation.

Compare marketing exposure and prescribing with local treatment access, diversion and illicit supply over time. This helps test contribution without treating temporal association as a complete causal account or ignoring decisions by distributors, prescribers and regulators.

Independent economic evidence

CDC overdose and prescription data document a major opioid crisis; epidemiological research identifies multiple pathways, including prescribing, illicit supply and later synthetic opioids.

The plea findings concern specific marketing and reporting conduct. Epidemiology establishes a wider evolving overdose emergency with multiple causes, so the company’s contribution must be estimated by period and mechanism.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The company’s misconduct is not the sole cause of the epidemic. Causal estimates vary by place and period; distinguish company conduct, prescriber decisions, medical need, illicit markets and regulatory failures.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare dated marketing claims, training, prescribing patterns and overdose trends across exposed and less-exposed regions; test competing channels while controlling for later illicit supply.

Assessment: Misbranding and deceptive practices were admitted in defined cases; attributing the entire opioid epidemic to one company is unsupported.
