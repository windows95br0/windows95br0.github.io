FILE FCP-05 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Glass–Steagall and the separation of banking activities

ASSESSMENT: The 1933 law addressed real conflicts; the single-cause repeal narrative is unsupported.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether banking separation responded to documented conflicts or whether its later repeal single-handedly caused the 2008 crisis.

Glass–Steagall comprised several provisions, including restrictions on securities affiliations and deposit insurance. Discussions that treat the whole act as one wall overlook its separate legal sections and the gradual agency interpretations that altered the boundary before 1999.

Context and timeline

After the 1929 crash and bank runs, Pecora hearings investigated bank securities practices. The Banking Act of 1933 created deposit insurance and restricted securities activity; the boundary was loosened and partially repealed in 1999.

The Kroszner–Rajan study examines pre-1933 securities affiliate performance, challenging the claim that all bank-underwritten issues were inferior. It does not erase conflicts of interest or decide whether deposit insurance should coexist with securities underwriting.

Primary records and formal findings

The statute and hearings document the reform and conflicts motivating it, but do not establish that all universal banking caused the Depression or that separation alone would have prevented it.

To assess repeal’s role in 2008, identify a channel directly enabled by the 1999 law and quantify its contribution relative to nonbank mortgage finance, securitization and short-term wholesale funding. A later crisis alone does not establish that causal link.

Independent economic evidence

Kroszner and Rajan find that pre-1933 commercial-bank affiliates did not uniformly issue lower-quality securities, complicating a simple causal story.

A causal claim about the 1999 repeal should identify the activity it enabled, the institution that undertook it and the path from that activity to loss. Crisis timing alone cannot supply those missing links.

Counter-evidence and limitations

2008 losses involved mortgage underwriting, securitization, repo finance and nonbanks beyond the old boundary. Repeal may have affected incentives without being a sufficient or sole cause.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare bank failure and risk data across business models; isolate exposures specifically enabled by repeal rather than attributing all later mortgage risk to it.

Assessment: The 1933 law addressed real conflicts; the single-cause repeal narrative is unsupported.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  2. Pecora Investigation hearings (1933–34)Document selected securities practices and testimony, not a representative sample of every bank.
  3. Randall S. Kroszner and Raghuram G. Rajan, “Is the Glass–Steagall Act Justified?” American Economic Review (1994)Empirical analysis challenges the claim that bank affiliates invariably issued inferior securities.
