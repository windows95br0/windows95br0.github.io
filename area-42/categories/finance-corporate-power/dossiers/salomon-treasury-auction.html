FILE FCP-08 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Salomon Brothers’ Treasury auction violations

ASSESSMENT: False customer bids and limit evasion are established; broader impact and senior knowledge remain bounded questions.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Salomon executives evaded Treasury auction limits by submitting false customer bids and cornering issues.

Treasury’s auction rules limited a bidder’s share of an issue, and false customer names enabled bids above that ceiling. That mechanism is established by firm records and investigations; whether top executives knew earlier than they admitted is a separate evidentiary question.

Context and timeline

In 1991, Salomon disclosed that a trader submitted false bids under customers’ names, letting the firm exceed auction limits. Executives resigned; Treasury revised auction procedures after investigations and congressional hearings.

The Brady joint report reviewed both primary auctions and the government-securities market after a confidence shock. It describes potential concentration and systemic concerns, but does not prove that Salomon moved every Treasury yield or manipulated the entire market.

Primary records and formal findings

The Treasury, Federal Reserve and SEC joint report documents unauthorized bids, control failures and rule changes. It distinguishes proven bidding from disputed scope of senior managers’ knowledge.

Customer authorization, bid timestamps and dealer inventory are the decisive checks. Compare auction allocations and subsequent prices with other bidders, and distinguish an improper bid from the separate question of measurable harm to the Treasury or other investors.

Independent economic evidence

Auction concentration and secondary-market prices show why control of scarce issues mattered; the Brady report analyzes systemic risk without establishing manipulation of all Treasury prices.

False customer bids violate the auction limit regardless of whether they changed all Treasury prices. Measure market impact independently and keep disputed management knowledge separate from the established conduct.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Not every executive had proven prior knowledge, and the firm’s disclosure preceded some findings. A scandal does not show that all government auctions were routinely rigged.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Audit bid authorizations, timestamps, holdings and when-issued prices. Determine whether customer orders were genuine and how much the concentration changed prices.

Assessment: False customer bids and limit evasion are established; broader impact and senior knowledge remain bounded questions.
