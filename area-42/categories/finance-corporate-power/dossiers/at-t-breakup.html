FILE FCP-38 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

AT&T’s antitrust case and the 1984 divestiture

ASSESSMENT: A serious exclusion case produced a structural remedy; the effect of breakup should be stated with service-specific evidence.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether AT&T’s integrated control of local telephone networks excluded competitors and justified structural separation.

The lawsuit focused on control of local exchange access and the separation between local and long-distance service. The consent decree’s negotiated structural remedy is evidence of a competition dispute resolved in court, but not a trial verdict accepting every DOJ allegation.

Context and timeline

The Justice Department sued AT&T in 1974. The 1982 consent decree separated local Bell operating companies from long-distance and equipment businesses, effective 1984; telecommunications competition and technology then changed rapidly.

Long-distance entry and equipment innovation followed divestiture, while local service remained regulated and network access continued to matter. Changes cannot be attributed to the decree alone without controlling for fiber, digital switching, wireless substitution and later regulatory reforms.

Primary records and formal findings

The DOJ complaint, consent decree and appellate decisions document allegations and negotiated remedies; a consent decree is not a full trial finding on every disputed fact.

A strong assessment separates the legal basis for the remedy from its economic performance. Compare service-specific prices, quality and entry, and account for the decree’s eventual modification; do not infer that every large integrated network is necessarily anticompetitive.

Independent economic evidence

Economic histories of telecom competition examine network effects, interconnection costs, innovation and price changes after divestiture; results differ by service and period.

The litigation establishes a particular competition remedy; evaluating the remedy requires a separate account of entry, rates and quality in each telecommunications market and of the technological changes that followed.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The breakup cannot be credited with every later innovation or price reduction; technology, regulation and demand also changed. Nor does the case establish that all large integrated firms are illegal monopolies.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare prices, service quality and entry before and after divestiture, adjusting for technology and regulation; inspect interconnection terms for discriminatory access.

Assessment: A serious exclusion case produced a structural remedy; the effect of breakup should be stated with service-specific evidence.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  2. United States v. AT&T, 552 F. Supp. 131 (D.D.C. 1982)Court reviews public-interest basis for decree. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
  3. Robert W. Crandall, After the Breakup: U.S. Telecommunications in a More Competitive Era (1991)Independent economic assessment of competition and outcomes. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
