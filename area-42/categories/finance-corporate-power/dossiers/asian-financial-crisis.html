FILE FCP-28 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The Asian financial crisis and IMF conditionality

ASSESSMENT: Program design warrants criticism, but interacting domestic fragility, capital flows and external shocks explain the crisis better than a single plot.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether IMF austerity deepened Asian contractions or restored confidence after pegs and foreign borrowing failed.

The baht’s July 1997 float preceded contagion, while Korea’s late-year liquidity crunch and Indonesia’s banking collapse had different domestic structures. Treating them as one event obscures how short-term foreign-currency debt and weak supervision varied across countries.

Context and timeline

Thailand abandoned its baht peg in July 1997; stress spread to Indonesia and Korea. IMF rescue programs combined financing with fiscal, monetary and structural conditions amid bank failures and currency depreciation.

The debate over initial fiscal tightening is narrower than the broader question of IMF responsibility. Program terms changed, and the IEO’s retrospective examined implementation; private capital reversals, fixed-rate regimes and domestic financial weakness were already in play.

Primary records and formal findings

The IMF Independent Evaluation Office documents assumptions, conditions and changes. Country records establish crises and policy errors, not a uniform finding that the IMF caused every collapse.

Output and credit comparisons can reveal costs, but a counterfactual must account for exchange-rate collapse, bank closures and political uncertainty. The evidence supports criticism of choices and timing, not the inference that crisis conditions were deliberately imposed.

Independent economic evidence

Radelet and Sachs emphasize panic and tightening; Corsetti, Pesenti and Roubini stress financial fragility and moral hazard. Cross-country results vary.

Country-by-country evidence is essential: Thailand’s peg, Indonesian banking and Korean external refinancing were related but not interchangeable crises. Policy judgments should be evaluated against each country’s reserve and bank data.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Governments faced falling currencies, weak banks and low reserves. IMF programs may have stabilized some outcomes while imposing costs; criticism is not evidence of intentional impoverishment.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare program targets, output, bank credit and matched countries; identify changed conditions and whether liquidity arrived before or after capital flight.

Assessment: Program design warrants criticism, but interacting domestic fragility, capital flows and external shocks explain the crisis better than a single plot.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. IMF Independent Evaluation Office, The IMF and Recent Capital Account Crises (2003)Retrospective review of Indonesia, Korea and Brazil, noting counterfactual limits.
  2. Steven Radelet and Jeffrey Sachs, “The Onset of the East Asian Financial Crisis,” NBER WP 6680 (1998)Presents liquidity-panic interpretation. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
  3. Giancarlo Corsetti, Paolo Pesenti and Nouriel Roubini, “What Caused the Asian Currency and Financial Crisis?” Japan and the World Economy (1999)Alternative account emphasizes balance-sheet vulnerability. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
