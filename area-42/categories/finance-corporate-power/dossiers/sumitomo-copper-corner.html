FILE FCP-34 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Sumitomo’s copper trading scandal and market control claims

ASSESSMENT: Major unauthorized trading and market abuse are documented; board-wide foreknowledge and total price control are not.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether trader Yasuo Hamanaka concealed unauthorized trades and manipulated copper prices through Sumitomo’s position.

Hamanaka’s trading and false documentation concealed exposure from Sumitomo and market counterparties. The key question is how much copper he could control through exchange and off-exchange positions, not whether one trader literally owned the global supply.

Context and timeline

Hamanaka traded copper for Sumitomo for years, accumulating a large position and reported losses. In 1996, the company disclosed unauthorized activity; investigations found forged documents and misleading controls, while copper prices and inventory claims drew scrutiny.

CFTC enforcement and the Senate inquiry illuminate different parts of the episode: U.S. market conduct and regulatory oversight. Sumitomo’s public loss disclosure establishes corporate exposure but does not establish that its board approved the conduct in advance.

Primary records and formal findings

CFTC and U.S. court actions document false statements and manipulation-related claims; Sumitomo acknowledged losses and paid settlements. Legal outcomes focus on particular conduct, not every copper-price fluctuation.

Price effects should be tested against LME inventories, warrants and positions alongside demand and supply news. A concentrated position can distort a market without controlling every transaction or explaining all price movements across the period.

Independent economic evidence

LME warehouse stocks, futures positions and price series provide independent tests for cornering; market studies find concentrated positions can affect thin commodity markets.

Hidden positions and false records are distinct from the degree of market power achieved. Exchange inventory and warrant data can estimate price influence without claiming total control of global copper.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The size and duration of the hidden exposure were disputed, and a trader’s unauthorized positions do not prove corporate board knowledge. Global supply and demand also influenced copper.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Reconcile exchange reports, warehouse warrants, customer positions and internal limits; test price effects against inventory and demand changes while identifying the timing of concealed trades.

Assessment: Major unauthorized trading and market abuse are documented; board-wide foreknowledge and total price control are not.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  2. U.S. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, The Sumitomo Copper Case (1996)Investigates market oversight and positions. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
