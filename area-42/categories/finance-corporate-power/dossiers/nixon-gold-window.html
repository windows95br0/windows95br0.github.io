FILE FCP-24 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The 1971 suspension of dollar-gold convertibility

ASSESSMENT: The policy break and distributional effects are documented; a secret seizure of financial assets is not.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Nixon’s suspension was a secret default engineered to enrich a hidden financial network.

The announcement suspended official foreign conversion, not ordinary private domestic dollar exchange for gold. The move was part of a public package including a temporary import surcharge and wage-price controls, followed by negotiations that yielded new parities but no durable return to convertibility.

Context and timeline

Bretton Woods promised official dollar-gold conversion. U.S. deficits and overseas dollar balances rose as gold reserves faced pressure. Nixon suspended convertibility on August 15, 1971; negotiations failed to restore the old system.

Balance-of-payments pressure and foreign dollar claims are observable in reserve and diplomatic records. The Triffin problem describes the tension between supplying international liquidity and maintaining confidence in a fixed gold promise; it is not evidence of an undisclosed group controlling the decision.

Primary records and formal findings

Nixon’s address, Treasury and Federal Reserve records, and Smithsonian Agreement document the policy and negotiations as a public announcement amid reserve pressure.

Assess distributional consequences through exchange rates, inflation and reserve assets, but distinguish those outcomes from motive. Declassified policy records identify debate and negotiation; a hidden seizure claim would require evidence contrary to the public terms and recorded process.

Independent economic evidence

Bordo’s historical work and international reserve data explain the Triffin conflict between global liquidity and confidence in convertibility; pressure predated the decision.

The suspension was publicly announced and followed by recorded negotiations. The evidence supports a structural monetary break and distributional consequences, not a covert confiscation theory.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The policy changed international wealth and served domestic goals, but beneficiaries do not prove hidden intent. Historians debate repair alternatives and political motives.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare gold claims, reserve movements and declassified memoranda. A secret-coordination theory needs evidence beyond the documented White House and Treasury process.

Assessment: The policy break and distributional effects are documented; a secret seizure of financial assets is not.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. Richard Nixon, Address to the Nation (August 15, 1971)Primary announcement gives the administration’s rationale. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
  2. FRUS, Foreign Economic Policy, 1969–76, Vol. IIIDeclassified records document negotiations and internal disagreement. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
  3. Michael D. Bordo, “The Bretton Woods International Monetary System” (1993)Academic analysis explains reserve and convertibility pressures. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
