FILE FCP-33 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Sweden’s early-1990s banking crisis and guarantee

ASSESSMENT: Deregulation and property lending helped cause crisis; the state intervention protected system functions while imposing equity losses.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether deregulation and property lending produced a crisis and whether the state rescue protected bank owners.

Credit deregulation preceded a lending boom concentrated in commercial property; falling collateral values and recession then exposed bank losses. The 1992 blanket guarantee aimed to prevent runs, while equity holders were not simply protected from loss.

Context and timeline

Credit controls were removed in the 1980s, lending and commercial property prices surged, and recession and tax changes contributed to defaults. Sweden guaranteed bank liabilities in 1992, took stakes and imposed losses on shareholders.

Swedish resolution is often cited as a model because assistance was paired with asset review, public disclosure and restructuring. Its results depended on administrative capacity and fiscal credibility; it does not prove the same program would work identically in every banking system.

Primary records and formal findings

Government commission records and resolution accounts document support and nationalization; they show shareholder losses alongside creditor guarantees rather than a simple owner bailout.

Compare loan-loss recognition, asset transfers, state equity stakes and eventual recoveries to determine the net public cost. Separate the guarantee’s stabilization effect from the earlier policy failures that produced fragile banks.

Independent economic evidence

Lars Jonung and Peter Englund analyze credit deregulation, real estate and policy response; bank loan-loss data connect property values to insolvency.

The guarantee stabilized liabilities while shareholder losses and bank restructuring constrained support. The case is evidence for a resolution design, not proof that intervention had no fiscal risk.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The guarantee helped stabilize payments but shifted contingent risk to the state. Sweden’s relatively transparent resolution differs from later crises, and its success is not proof every country could copy it without cost.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare assistance with bank valuations, shareholder returns, creditor recoveries and fiscal outlays; test who bore losses under resolution rules.

Assessment: Deregulation and property lending helped cause crisis; the state intervention protected system functions while imposing equity losses.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. Swedish National Archives, NAD catalogue records for the Banking Crisis Commission (1990s)Official archival catalogue for identifying commission holdings and related crisis records.
  2. Peter Englund, “The Swedish Banking Crisis: Roots and Consequences,” Oxford Review of Economic Policy (1999)Academic causal analysis of property and credit. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
  3. Lars Jonung, “The Swedish Model for Resolving the Banking Crisis of 1991–93” (2009)Reviews guarantee and resolution design. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
