FILE FCP-39 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

United States v. Microsoft and browser tying

ASSESSMENT: Exclusionary conduct was legally established in specific practices; universal claims of technology suppression exceed the judgment.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Microsoft unlawfully used Windows dominance to restrict browser competition and protect its operating-system monopoly.

The appellate court affirmed core findings that Microsoft maintained its operating-system monopoly through exclusionary conduct, but reversed the breakup remedy and remanded remedy questions. The final consent judgment imposed conduct rules rather than corporate separation.

Context and timeline

The Justice Department and states sued in 1998, alleging exclusion of Netscape and restrictions on PC manufacturers. The district court found monopolization and proposed a breakup; the D.C. Circuit affirmed key findings but reversed the remedy, leading to settlement.

Browser distribution involved OEM contracts, technical integration and network effects. The case was not simply about bundling a product: the legal issue was whether specific conduct maintained monopoly power and harmed competition under the applicable rule-of-reason framework.

Primary records and formal findings

Findings of fact, appellate judgment and DOJ settlement establish specific monopolization and exclusionary conduct under antitrust law. They do not establish that all Microsoft products or later market actions were unlawful.

Internet Explorer and Netscape’s later market trajectories reflect product change, distribution and new entrants. They can test outcomes but do not retroactively replace the court’s evidence about conduct and market conditions during the litigated period.

Independent economic evidence

Bresnahan and other economists assess network effects, browser distribution and software innovation; market-share changes and entry provide evidence but do not isolate every remedy effect.

The appellate findings and final conduct judgment define the legal scope. Later market evolution can inform remedy evaluation, but cannot transform the case into a finding against all software integration.

Counter-evidence and limitations

The browser market changed with technology and consumer behavior, making long-term remedy effects uncertain. Settlement was not a full trial of every allegation, and legal findings should be tied to the appellate record.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Examine OEM contracts, technical restrictions, browser distribution and entry rates; test whether conduct foreclosed viable rivals beyond product quality and network effects.

Assessment: Exclusionary conduct was legally established in specific practices; universal claims of technology suppression exceed the judgment.
