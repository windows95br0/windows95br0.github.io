FILE FCP-72 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The Secret 1910 Jekyll Island Meeting and the Creation of the Federal Reserve

ASSESSMENT: A genuinely secret 1910 meeting of bankers and a senator drafted the plan that became the Federal Reserve; that documented secrecy is distinct from the unproven claim that the Fed was designed as a private banking cartel to control the U.S. economy for its founders' benefit.

Documented findings, institutional history and unresolved political claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

["The question is whether the Federal Reserve System was founded through an illegitimate, secret conspiracy of private bankers to seize control of the U.S. money supply for their own benefit, as popularized by G. Edward Griffin's book “The Creature from Jekyll Island.”", "The underlying 1910 meeting itself is not in dispute and is documented by the Federal Reserve's own historical archives; what is contested is the meeting's motive and the resulting institution's legitimacy and beneficiaries."]

Context and timeline

["In late November 1910, Senator Nelson Aldrich convened a small group — including Assistant Treasury Secretary A. Piatt Andrew, and bankers Henry Davison (J.P. Morgan & Co.), Frank Vanderlip (National City Bank), Paul Warburg (Kuhn, Loeb & Co.), and Benjamin Strong — at the secluded, privately owned Jekyll Island Club off the coast of Georgia. Participants reportedly used only first names and maintained a cover story of a duck-hunting trip to avoid press attention, a genuinely covert arrangement documented by several participants' later memoirs.", 'The group drafted the “Aldrich Plan” for a centralized reserve banking system to prevent recurring financial panics like that of 1907. The Aldrich Plan itself was never enacted as written; it was substantially revised through open congressional debate over the following three years before President Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act into law in December 1913, creating a system with more distributed regional structure and government appointees than the original private-banker-drafted plan.']

Primary records and formal findings

["The Federal Reserve's own historical archive and multiple independent bank-history accounts confirm the meeting's secrecy and its role as the direct conceptual precursor to the Federal Reserve Act, establishing the documented, undisputed core of the claim.", 'The final enacted Federal Reserve Act differed meaningfully from the original private Aldrich Plan: it created a Federal Reserve Board of presidentially appointed, Senate-confirmed governors with public accountability, and a system of twelve regional reserve banks rather than a single centralized private bank as some earlier drafts proposed — a structural compromise reached through two-plus years of public congressional hearings and revisions, not enacted in secret.']

Independent economic evidence

["Historians broadly agree that the secrecy at Jekyll Island reflected genuine political necessity: centralized banking proposals were deeply unpopular after the Panic of 1907 exposed Wall Street's role in economic instability, and any proposal publicly associated with major bankers risked being rejected outright by a suspicious Congress and public. This is a different, more mundane, motive than a deliberate plan to defraud the public.", '“The Creature from Jekyll Island” and similar accounts argue the resulting system nonetheless serves private banking interests at public expense through mechanisms like fractional-reserve lending and interest-rate policy; this is a legitimate, ongoing subject of economic policy debate (addressed by mainstream monetary economists on its own terms), distinct from the narrower historical claim of a secret cartel conspiracy at the founding.']

Counter-evidence and limitations

["Attribution should stay bounded: the meeting's secrecy, attendee list and role in drafting the Aldrich Plan are documented historical fact; whether the resulting, substantially revised and publicly debated Federal Reserve Act constitutes an ongoing “cartel” benefiting its architects is a separate economic-policy argument that has not been established as historical fact by the same documentary standard.", "No surviving primary record from the participants or contemporary correspondence supports a specific plan to defraud the public financially, as opposed to a policy goal (however self-interested some participants' motives may also have been) of preventing further bank panics."]

Falsifiable checks and assessment

["Documentary evidence such as unreleased personal correspondence among the Jekyll Island attendees explicitly describing an intent to defraud the public, or peer-reviewed economic analysis establishing a specific measurable transfer of wealth attributable to the Federal Reserve's structure as designed at Jekyll Island (rather than to its subsequent century of policy decisions under public oversight), would materially change this assessment. General distrust of central banking policy is a separate, legitimate debate that this file does not attempt to resolve."]
