FILE FCP-06 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The savings-and-loan crisis and regulatory forbearance

ASSESSMENT: Regulatory delay and specific fraud worsened the crisis; a single organized scheme to transfer all losses is unsupported.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether deregulation, fraud and delayed closure turned thrift losses into a taxpayer bailout, and whether this was a coordinated scheme.

Thrifts funded long-duration fixed-rate mortgages with deposits whose costs reset rapidly as interest rates rose. The resulting negative spreads were a real macroeconomic shock; expanded powers and deposit insurance then created incentives for some insolvent institutions to gamble for recovery.

Context and timeline

High interest rates squeezed thrifts holding long-term mortgages. Deregulation expanded investment powers while supervision lagged; real-estate losses and fraud accumulated. FIRREA created the Resolution Trust Corporation in 1989.

RTC closures eventually limited continued risk-taking, but delayed recognition allowed losses and fraud to grow in some institutions. GAO and FDIC estimates differ with interest, recoveries and timing, so a cost figure should name its accounting basis.

Primary records and formal findings

FIRREA, GAO audits and RTC records establish failed institutions, resolution costs and supervisory shortcomings. Enforcement cases establish fraud by specific owners and executives, not all policymakers or thrifts.

Institution-level exam reports, closure dates and asset files can separate interest-rate losses from speculative real-estate bets and fraud. A generalized bailout claim should identify who received funds, which creditors were protected and what shareholders lost.

Independent economic evidence

FDIC economists Curry and Shibut separate interest-rate losses, asset risk, fraud and delayed closure, showing moral hazard without a centrally organized plot.

Interest-rate mismatch, later risk-taking and delayed closure contributed in different proportions across thrifts. Cost claims should state how recoveries and timing are counted and should not label all losses fraud.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Ultimate public cost varies by accounting assumptions. Deregulation alone is not sufficient: macroeconomic shocks, deposit guarantees, local property cycles and political pressure interacted.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare closure dates, examiner warnings, asset quality and political contacts institution by institution; demonstrate unequal intervention before alleging connected-owner favoritism.

Assessment: Regulatory delay and specific fraud worsened the crisis; a single organized scheme to transfer all losses is unsupported.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (1989)Primary statute created the RTC and changed supervision; not a causal audit.
  2. U.S. GAO, Thrift Industry: Cost of the Government’s Assistance (1996)Official cost review explains resolution estimates and assumptions. Its findings are limited to the parties, conduct, dates or mandate covered by that record.
  3. Steven A. Curry and Lynn Shibut, “The Cost of the Savings and Loan Crisis,” FDIC Banking Review (2000)Independent agency analysis apportions costs among causes. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
