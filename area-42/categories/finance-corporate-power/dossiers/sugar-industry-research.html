FILE FCP-47 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Sugar industry funding and the 1960s heart-disease review

ASSESSMENT: Undisclosed industry influence on one review is documented; claims of total control over nutrition science overstate the record.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether sugar-industry funding influenced a published review that emphasized dietary fat and minimized sugar-related concerns.

The 1967 review was written after the Sugar Research Foundation funded the project, and archival correspondence records sponsor involvement in defining its scope and receiving drafts. That supports a conflict and influence finding for this publication, not proof of a field-wide agreement.

Context and timeline

The Sugar Research Foundation funded Harvard researchers to review coronary-heart-disease literature. A 1967 New England Journal of Medicine review discussed sugar and fat; later archival research found funder communications and undisclosed sponsorship.

Contemporary nutrition science debated dietary fat, sugar and coronary disease with limited evidence compared with later studies. The historical review should be judged against then-available research, while sponsor communications show why disclosure and editorial independence were important.

Primary records and formal findings

Kearns, Schmidt and Glantz’s 2016 JAMA Internal Medicine article reproduces archival correspondence showing the sponsor shaped project scope and review conclusions. It does not prove the funding dictated all later nutrition policy.

Trace which studies were included or excluded and whether later guidelines relied on the review. Demonstrating influence on a paper is distinct from proving that it changed national policy or that sugar alone caused the disease burden.

Independent economic evidence

Historical nutrition science and subsequent cohort and metabolic research independently test the relative dietary risks of sugar and fat; the evidence base evolved beyond the 1967 review.

Archival correspondence is strongest evidence of sponsor influence on a particular literature review. Demonstrating downstream policy impact requires tracing citations and decision records beyond the paper itself.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Funding influence is documented, but the episode does not establish that sugar alone caused heart disease or that the entire field was controlled. The authors’ conclusions and later policies had multiple sources.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare the review’s included studies and wording with the full literature and sponsor correspondence; trace citations into guidelines and test whether omitted evidence changed recommendations.
