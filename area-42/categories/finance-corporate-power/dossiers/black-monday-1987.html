FILE FCP-36 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The 1987 stock-market crash and portfolio insurance

ASSESSMENT: Feedback trading and market design failures contributed; deliberate orchestration is unsupported.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether computerized trading or coordinated insiders intentionally crashed global markets.

The Brady Task Force described portfolio insurance as a feedback strategy: falling cash prices prompted futures sales, which could widen discounts and prompt more selling. This mechanism is testable with timestamped order and futures-basis data, not inferred merely from the scale of the decline.

Context and timeline

On October 19, 1987, U.S. stocks fell sharply after earlier declines in other markets. Portfolio insurance and index futures selling interacted with illiquidity and delayed trading; circuit-breaker and market structure reforms followed.

International trading hours and market closures complicate any claim about a single cause. The crash followed earlier price weakness, and market structure differed across countries; portfolio insurance amplified stress in some accounts but was not a centrally coordinated sell order.

Primary records and formal findings

The Presidential Task Force (Brady) report and SEC studies document trading mechanisms, market breaks and oversight gaps, not a coordinated plot to crash markets.

Later circuit breakers and coordinated trading halts reflect lessons about liquidity and information transmission. To claim intentional crashing would require evidence of an agreement or deceptive orders beyond the documented mechanical feedback and thin-market conditions.

Independent economic evidence

Richard Roll and later empirical studies compare international timing, futures discounts and portfolio-insurance flows to test contagion and feedback effects.

The key measurable mechanism is feedback between portfolio-insurance sales, futures discounts and cash-market liquidity. It accounts for amplification without converting the crash into evidence of coordinated intent.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Portfolio insurance likely amplified selling but cannot fully explain the initial global shock. Large losses and synchronized prices do not establish intentional action; causal estimates differ.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Reconstruct order books, futures basis, insurance positions and communications; test whether selling preceded price declines or responded to them, and compare markets with different rules.

Assessment: Feedback trading and market design failures contributed; deliberate orchestration is unsupported.
