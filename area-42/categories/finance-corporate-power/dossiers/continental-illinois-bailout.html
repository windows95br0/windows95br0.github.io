FILE FCP-07 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Continental Illinois and the 1984 too-big-to-fail intervention

ASSESSMENT: The rescue and policy consequences are documented; a secret bank-government pact is not.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether the federal rescue of Continental Illinois established preferential protection for large banks at smaller competitors’ expense.

Continental’s rescue preserved payment and correspondent relationships while diluting or displacing equity ownership. That distinction is crucial: federal protection of many creditors did not mean the bank’s shareholders were made whole.

Context and timeline

Continental expanded commercial lending and bought energy-sector loan participations. Oil-related losses and depositor concern triggered a run in May 1984; regulators supplied liquidity and capital, then the FDIC assumed control.

The term “too big to fail” captured the perception that large-bank creditors could receive different protection, but one emergency decision cannot measure a durable funding subsidy. Estimates must separate expected support from ordinary differences in size, credit quality and business mix.

Primary records and formal findings

GAO and FDIC records detail assistance and decision-making. They establish a rescue and unequal creditor treatment concerns, not a pre-crisis secret agreement with the bank.

The contemporaneous GAO account is useful for what officials did and knew at the time; later FDIC histories assess lessons with hindsight. Compare their stated contagion rationale with the actual allocation of losses and the treatment of similarly situated institutions.

Independent economic evidence

Later funding-cost studies identify a possible large-bank subsidy through depositor expectations; estimates depend on credit-risk controls and the definition of implicit guarantee.

The rescue’s distribution should be described by creditor class: shareholders, uninsured creditors, depositors and the public did not necessarily receive the same treatment. This is a more precise test of bailout claims than the label “too big to fail.”

Counter-evidence and limitations

Protection of creditors did not mean shareholders were preserved: equity lost control and value. Rescue can reduce contagion while increasing future moral hazard, and one case cannot quantify a permanent subsidy.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare borrowing costs before and after 1984 across bank sizes while controlling for risk; inspect regulator communications to distinguish systemic rationale from favoritism.

Assessment: The rescue and policy consequences are documented; a secret bank-government pact is not.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. U.S. GAO, Continental Illinois National Bank: The Federal Government’s Response (1984)Contemporaneous oversight details intervention and its rationale. Its findings are limited to the parties, conduct, dates or mandate covered by that record.
  2. FDIC, History of the Eighties—Lessons for the Future, Vol. I (1997)Agency retrospective traces resolution policy and hindsight. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
  3. Gary H. Stern and Ron J. Feldman, Too Big to Fail (2004)Independent policy analysis explains moral hazard, not a secret deal.
