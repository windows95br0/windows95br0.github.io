FILE FCP-32 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Japan’s late-1980s asset bubble and monetary policy

ASSESSMENT: The bubble, tightening and slow cleanup are documented; intentional destruction or foreign orchestration is not.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Japanese authorities deliberately inflated then burst asset prices to benefit banks or foreign interests.

Land and equity prices rose alongside rapid credit growth and collateral-based lending, making rising valuations reinforce loan expansion. Tightening from 1989 and the subsequent price reversal exposed bad loans, but the loss recognition and balance-sheet repair took years.

Context and timeline

After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the yen appreciated. Monetary easing and credit expansion coincided with rising land and equity prices; the Bank of Japan tightened in 1989–90, and prices fell sharply thereafter.

The Bank of Japan research reconstructs policy choices; Hoshi and Kashyap focus on banks and corporate finance. Together, these sources distinguish the initial bubble from the later credit crunch and explain why a short rate increase alone cannot account for a decade of stagnation.

Primary records and formal findings

Bank of Japan historical records and official financial-system reviews document rate changes, lending and later nonperforming-loan recognition. They do not find a secret plan to crash the market.

Claims of intentional boom-and-bust require dated evidence that authorities sought the collapse, not just evidence that policy was late or misjudged. Compare meeting records and supervisory files with lending standards, property prices and the timing of loan recognition.

Independent economic evidence

Hoshi and Kashyap examine bank lending and corporate finance; land-price and equity series quantify the boom and prolonged balance-sheet repair.

The bubble and subsequent bad-loan overhang were distinct phases. Lending incentives and delayed recognition explain persistence more directly than claims that policy-makers intended a crash.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Policy timing and regulatory forbearance are debated. An asset bubble can result from credit incentives, expectations and delayed tightening without proving that authorities intended the crash.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare credit standards, collateral values, supervisory warnings and BOJ minutes across the boom and bust; determine whether institutions knowingly postponed recognition to protect connected lenders.

Assessment: The bubble, tightening and slow cleanup are documented; intentional destruction or foreign orchestration is not.
