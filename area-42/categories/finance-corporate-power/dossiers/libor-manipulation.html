FILE FCP-19 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

LIBOR submissions and documented rate manipulation

ASSESSMENT: Named traders and banks manipulated rates; a universal, timeless LIBOR conspiracy is not established.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether traders coordinated false submissions to benefit derivatives positions or make banks appear healthier.

LIBOR relied on a panel estimate of borrowing cost, leaving room for both trader influence and legitimate uncertainty when unsecured term borrowing nearly disappeared. Separate requests linked to positions from crisis submissions motivated by reputational concerns.

Context and timeline

LIBOR used panel banks’ borrowing-cost estimates rather than direct transaction averages. Investigators found trader requests for favorable rates and crisis-era pressure to submit lower borrowing costs; multiple banks faced enforcement.

CFTC orders and criminal pleas establish particular communications and submissions. The later Wheatley review addressed benchmark design and reform; it does not expand the legal findings to banks or dates not covered by enforcement actions.

Primary records and formal findings

CFTC orders, DOJ pleas and the Wheatley Review document manipulation by specific traders and institutions during defined periods, not all banks or tenors at all times.

Empirical comparisons with overnight indexed swaps and unsecured funding can reveal anomalous submissions, but different instruments price different credit, term and liquidity risks. Coordinated messages and trader incentives make the strongest evidence of intent.

Independent economic evidence

Snider and Youle and later market studies compare submitted rates with observable funding costs, testing systematic deviations and benchmark incentives.

The record supports manipulation in bounded episodes. Submission anomalies should be interpreted alongside the disappearance of unsecured term transactions and evidence of trader communications.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Crisis-era low submissions could reflect both reputation incentives and scarce unsecured borrowing. A bank difference alone does not prove collusion; legal findings have institutional and date limits.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare submissions with funding transactions, credit spreads, trader messages and positions. Require communications or coordinated patterns beyond shared responses to market stress.

Assessment: Named traders and banks manipulated rates; a universal, timeless LIBOR conspiracy is not established.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. CFTC, Barclays LIBOR order (2012)Documents trader requests and submissions in defined periods.
  2. DOJ, UBS Securities Japan guilty plea and LIBOR cases (2012–15)Criminal records establish admissions by named firms. Its findings are limited to the parties, conduct, dates or mandate covered by that record.
  3. David Snider and Thomas Youle, “Does LIBOR Reflect Banks’ Borrowing Costs?” (2010)Independent empirical test with acknowledged data limitations. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
