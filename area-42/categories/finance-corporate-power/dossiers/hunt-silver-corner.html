FILE FCP-35 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

The Hunt brothers’ attempted silver corner

ASSESSMENT: Concentrated speculative activity and market disruption are well documented; total control of silver prices is an exaggeration.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether concentrated futures and physical silver positions drove the 1980 spike and crash.

The Hunt positions combined physical metal and futures, so concentration must be measured against deliverable supply rather than total above-ground silver. Futures margin rules and delivery obligations shaped how positions translated into pressure on the exchange.

Context and timeline

Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt amassed silver and futures positions as inflation and investment demand rose. Exchange margin and position rules tightened; prices peaked in January 1980 and plunged on “Silver Thursday” in March.

Exchange “liquidation only” measures and higher margins altered market mechanics in early 1980. The resulting forced selling is documented, but does not prove a government-directed action or that the Hunts controlled all silver available to industry.

Primary records and formal findings

CFTC investigations, exchange rule records and civil court proceedings document concentrated holdings, rule changes and market disruption. They establish an attempted corner and rule violations, not that the Hunts alone controlled all supply or price.

Compare exchange warehouse stocks, contract concentration and physical premiums with inflation expectations and industrial demand. These measures can estimate cornering influence, but a contested counterfactual price remains more uncertain than the documented positions.

Independent economic evidence

Futures prices, warehouse stocks and margin changes allow tests of position impact; commodity economists distinguish fundamental demand from leverage and forced liquidation.

Position concentration and exchange-rule changes are documented; the price effect needs comparison with deliverable supply, other metals and inflation news. The existence of a corner attempt is narrower than total control.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Inflation fears, industrial demand and exchange rule changes also shaped prices. The later crash is not proof that all silver supply was secretly withheld, nor do losses establish a government conspiracy.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Match positions to deliverable inventories, margin calls and intraday price changes; compare with other metals and macro news to isolate concentration effects.

Assessment: Concentrated speculative activity and market disruption are well documented; total control of silver prices is an exaggeration.
