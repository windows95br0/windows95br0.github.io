FILE FCP-15 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

Credit-rating conflicts in the mortgage securities boom

ASSESSMENT: Conflicts and severe analytical failures are documented; deliberate fraud must be proved rating by rating.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether issuer-paid rating conflicts and weak diligence produced inflated ratings on mortgage securities and CDOs.

Ratings were embedded in securitization and regulatory capital frameworks, making errors consequential even where investors knew agencies were paid by issuers. The relevant comparison is not only rating versus default, but the assumptions available at origination and how model governance responded to deteriorating loans.

Context and timeline

Originators pooled mortgage loans into securities and CDOs. Rating agencies competed for issuer business as loan quality weakened; downgrades accelerated from 2007, creating losses across banks, funds and insurers.

The SEC’s 2008 examination sampled agencies and identified process failures; it was not a finding that every rating was fraudulent. FCIC commissioners disagreed about the crisis’s broader causes, which cautions against treating one official report as a unanimous causal verdict.

Primary records and formal findings

The FCIC and SEC documented model failures, inadequate resources and business conflicts, but did not prove every rating was knowingly false.

Loan-level performance and rating revisions provide independent outcome evidence; internal committee records are needed for intent. Test whether contemporaneous default data contradicted the model, and distinguish negligent overconfidence from knowing misrepresentation.

Independent economic evidence

Benmelech and Dlugosz analyze CDO ratings; loan-performance and downgrade data independently show default assumptions were too optimistic as underwriting weakened.

Model failure, conflicts and intent are distinct questions. Issuance-date loan data test whether assumptions were unreasonable; internal records are required to show knowing misrepresentation.

Counter-evidence and limitations

Extreme model failure is not automatically intentional fraud. Investors had varying access to loan data and knowingly relied on ratings to differing degrees; the relative causes remain contested.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Compare loan-level defaults known at issuance with model assumptions, committee minutes and fee incentives. Intent requires evidence analysts knew assumptions were indefensible when ratings were assigned.

Assessment: Conflicts and severe analytical failures are documented; deliberate fraud must be proved rating by rating.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, Final Report (2011)Official inquiry documents rating failures and conflicts; includes disagreement among commissioners. Its findings are limited to the parties, conduct, dates or mandate covered by that record.
  2. SEC, Summary Report of Issues Identified in Rating Agency Examinations (2008)Regulatory review finds process and model weaknesses in a limited sample.
  3. Efraim Benmelech and Jennifer Dlugosz, “The Credit Rating Crisis,” NBER Macroeconomics Annual (2009)Academic analysis of CDO structure and downgrades. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
