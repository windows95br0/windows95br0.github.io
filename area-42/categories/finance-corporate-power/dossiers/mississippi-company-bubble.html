FILE FCP-02 / HISTORICAL CASE FILE

John Law’s Mississippi Company and France’s debt crisis

ASSESSMENT: Expansion and collapse are documented; a wholly knowing confidence trick is not.

Documented findings, economic interpretation and unresolved claims are assessed separately.

Claim under review

The question is whether Banque Royale and the Mississippi Company deliberately used paper money and colonial privileges to conceal France’s fiscal insolvency.

Law’s institutions combined a note-issuing bank, tax collection, debt conversion and colonial-company privileges. That concentration meant the state’s fiscal credibility and the currency’s credibility became linked, so doubt in one institution could affect the other.

Context and timeline

After 1715, Law’s bank and trading company absorbed tax and monopoly privileges and public debt. Notes and subscriptions expanded; confidence broke in 1720, followed by restrictions on redemption.

The Banque Royale’s note issue and company share price were not a simple one-to-one claim on colonial profits. The relevant record includes exchange rates, share subscriptions, redemption decrees and fiscal transfers; later accounts sometimes overstate the role of one commodity or one announcement.

Primary records and formal findings

Royal decrees and company records establish state-backed monetary expansion and the subsequent redemption limits; they do not establish a secret plan to make notes worthless.

Compare promised redemption with liquid reserves and political decisions as confidence weakened. A badly designed public monetary experiment is not necessarily fraudulent; intent requires evidence that officials knowingly made false representations about assets or redemption capacity.

Independent economic evidence

Antoin Murphy and Larry Neal explain the debt-conversion, liquidity and credibility mechanisms using monetary history and the market record.

The breakdown linked state debt, bank notes and company shares. To establish knowing fraud rather than failed monetary policy, one must show what redemption or asset representations officials knew to be false.

Counter-evidence and limitations

This was an overt policy experiment, not a hidden enterprise. Its collapse can reflect design error, political resistance and speculative behavior; it does not prove colonial trade was the only supposed source of value.

Falsifiable checks and assessment

Reconstruct notes outstanding, reserves, subscription receipts and redemption promises, then compare internal warnings with issuance and restriction dates.

Assessment: Expansion and collapse are documented; a wholly knowing confidence trick is not.

Sources and evidentiary notes

  1. Royal decrees and Banque Royale records (1716–1720)Primary documents show institutional design and redemption policy, but cannot alone establish intent.
  2. Antoin E. Murphy, John Law: Economic Theorist and Policy-Maker (1997)Scholarly biography weighs fiscal and monetary explanations. This work tests an economic interpretation; its estimates depend on data and assumptions and do not establish intent.
  3. Larry Neal, The Financial Crisis of 1720 (1990)Comparative analysis of the French and British bubbles cautions against one-cause accounts. It addresses this episode but cannot alone resolve the broader causal question.
